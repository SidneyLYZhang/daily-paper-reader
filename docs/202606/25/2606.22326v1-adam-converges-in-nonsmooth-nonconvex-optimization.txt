## Abstract 

Adam is one of the most widely implemented and influential modern optimizers. Why is it effective across different optimization problems in practice? This question arguably lies at the center of the optimization community over the last decade and has motivated a substantial body of work aimed at understanding its convergence behavior. However, existing studies have mainly focused on the convergence rate of Adam in smooth nonconvex optimization, which unfortunately does not adequately capture practical settings, since many real-world problems are nonsmooth, such as those arising in training neural networks. Thus, these studies cannot fully explain the popularity and empirical success of Adam .Recently, an insightful and powerful framework called Online-to-Nonconvex Conversion has opened a new way to analyze Adam for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. Unfortunately, prior works along this line share two common limitations. First, all of them ignore the important bias-correction term in the original Adam algorithm. Second and more importantly, many of them require extra operations that are not used in Adam , such as a clipping step. Therefore, the convergence guarantee for the original Adam method still remains unclear. In this work, we present the first finite-time analysis for the classical form of Adam , i.e., with the bias-correction step and without further algorithmic modifications, and prove that a randomly scaled learning rate ensures a convergence rate of 1/T 213 for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. Moreover, our result provably applies to the modern heavy-tailed noise regime, which is closer to practice. Interestingly, our theory is established under the parameter choice β1 = β2, aligning with the recent empirical studies. 

Keywords: Adam, nonsmooth nonconvex optimization, stochastic optimization, heavy-tailed noise, online learning 

## 1 Introduction 

Adam , a modern optimizer that builds upon the seminal adaptive method AdaGrad (McMahan and Streeter, 2010; Duchi et al., 2011) and the famous RMSProp algorithm (Tieleman et al., 2012), was introduced by Kingma and Ba (2014) in 2014. Since then, it has undoubtedly become one of the most widely implemented and influential optimization methods. Despite its strong empirical performance, one question has remained central to the optimization community over the last decade: 

Why is Adam effective across different optimization problems in practice? 

This question has motivated a substantial body of work aimed at understanding the convergence behavior, theoretical properties, and provable advantages of Adam (see, e.g., Reddi et al. (2018); Zaheer et al. (2018); De et al. (2018); Chen et al. (2019); Zou et al. (2019); Barakat and Bianchi (2021); Guo et al. (2021); Défossez et al. (2022); Zhang et al. (2022); Li et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2023, 2024); Zhang et al. (2025)). However, existing studies have primarily focused on the convergence of Adam for smooth non-convex optimization, which does not adequately capture practical settings, since many real-world objectives, such as those encountered in neural network training, are nonsmooth. Consequently,

these studies cannot fully explain the popularity and empirical success of Adam . Thus, develop-ing convergence guarantees for Adam beyond smooth functions is essential for building a theory that more faithfully reflects its practical behavior. This gap indicates that the existing theoretical understanding remains incomplete. Recently, an insightful and powerful framework, known as Online-to-Nonconvex Conversion (O2NC ) (Cutkosky et al., 2023), has opened a new avenue for analyzing Adam in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. Unfortunately, prior works along this line (Ahn et al., 2024; Ahn and Cutkosky, 2024; Nguyen, 2026; Xie et al., 2026) share two common limitations. First, all of them ignore the important bias-correction term in the original Adam algorithm, which is a key component in the default implementation of Adam . Second and more importantly, many of these works require additional operations that are not part of Adam , such as a clipping step. As a result, the algorithms analyzed in these works differ from the original Adam method. Therefore, the convergence guarantee for the original Adam method has yet to be established. 

1.1 Our Contributions 

In this work, we analyze the classical form of Adam , i.e., with bias correction and without any further algorithmic modifications, and establish the following results: 

• We prove that a randomly scaled learning rate ensures that Adam converges at a rate of 

1/T 213 in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. To the best of our knowledge, this gives the first finite-time rate for the original Adam algorithm in this setting. 

• Our analysis provably applies to the modern heavy-tailed noise regime, which more closely reflects practice (Simsekli et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020a). Specifically, when the tail index 

p of the gradient noise satisfies 4/3 < p ≤ 2, we show that Adam converges at a rate of 

1/T 2(3p −4) 19p −12 .

• Interestingly, our theory is established under the parameter choice β1 = β2, which aligns with the recent empirical studies (Orvieto and Gower, 2025). 

• Finally, we extend our results to the case where the problem-dependent parameters (e.g., the tail index p) are unknown. 

1.2 Related Work 

Adam . Adam was proposed by Kingma and Ba (2014) as a further generalization of RMSProp 

(Tieleman et al., 2012), both of which were inspired by the well-known adaptive algorithm AdaGrad 

(McMahan and Streeter, 2010; Duchi et al., 2011) originally designed for Online Convex Optimization (OCO). Since its introduction, Adam and its variants, such as AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019), have been observed to be highly effective across a wide range of optimization tasks and have become dominant tools in modern machine learning, especially for training neural networks. Two hyperparameters that are particularly important for Adam are β1 ∈ [0 , 1) and β2 ∈ [0 , 1) ,the exponential decay rates for the moment estimates. Kingma and Ba (2014) suggested setting 

β1 = 0 .9 and β2 = 0 .999 by default. However, in the literature on large language models (Brown et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a), these two parameters are often set to β1 = 0 .9

and β2 = 0 .95 . In addition, recent works, such as Orvieto and Gower (2025), suggest choosing 

β1 = β2 for improved performance, based on extensive empirical experiments. 

Online-to-Nonconvex Conversion. The Online-to-Nonconvex Conversion ( O2NC ) method, in-troduced by Cutkosky et al. (2023), provides a powerful framework for converting the regret bound of an OCO algorithm into a provable bound for nonconvex optimization. However, one undesirable feature of O2NC is that it queries the (stochastic) gradient at a new point that differs from the 

> 2Adam Converges in Nonsmooth Nonconvex Optimization

current iterate. To overcome this issue, Zhang and Cutkosky (2024) later proposed a new method called Exponentiated Online-to-Nonconvex Conversion ( EO2NC ). These two methods have since served as fundamental tools for studying nonconvex optimization, for both smooth and nonsmooth objectives, e.g., Liu et al. (2024b); Jiang et al. (2025); Ahn et al. (2025); Liu (2026c). More surprisingly, it is known that Adam (with a randomly scaled learning rate) can be derived from EO2NC when combined with the classical Follow-the-Regularized-Leader ( FTRL ) algorithm for OCO (Ahn et al., 2024; Ahn and Cutkosky, 2024). Recently, two more works (Nguyen, 2026; Xie et al., 2026) have followed this line of research and obtained further results. 

Nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. In the literature on machine learning, Zhang et al. (2020b) provided the first non-asymptotic analysis for finding stationary points of nonsmooth non-convex functions, where the stationary point is defined through the Goldstein δ-subdifferential (Gold-stein, 1977). Since then, several subsequent works have made further contributions (Davis et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2022; Kornowski and Shamir, 2022b; Jordan et al., 2023; Kornowski and Shamir, 2022a; Tian and So, 2024). Notably, the O2NC and EO2NC frameworks discussed above provide a systematic way to construct algorithms that guarantee optimal convergence rates (Cutkosky et al., 2023; Zhang and Cutkosky, 2024; Ahn and Cutkosky, 2024; Ahn et al., 2025). However, these works only consider deterministic gradient feedback or a stochastic gradient oracle with finite-variance noise. Such settings could not accurately capture practical scenarios, since the finite-variance condition, which also covers the deterministic setting, is often considered too optimistic; in contrast, heavy-tailed noise provides a more realistic model, where the stochastic noise has only a finite p-th moment for p ∈ (1 , 2] (Simsekli et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020a). Under heavy-tailed noise, using the O2NC framework, Liu et al. (2024b) proved the first high-probability convergence bound, and Liu (2026c) established the first optimal in-expectation rate with a matching lower bound.

## 5 Theoretical Analysis 

In this section, we provide the theoretical analysis and prove Theorem 1 at the end. Compared with the existing literature, our proofs require a more careful and technical analysis, since we neither restrict the domain of FTRL to a bounded set, which is equivalent to clipping δt, as in Ahn et al. (2024) and Ahn and Cutkosky (2024), nor add additional regularization to the loss function ℓt, which is equivalent to modifying the stepsize of Adam , as in Xie et al. (2026), and, moreover, we include the important bias-correction term that was, however, missed in all prior works studying Adam from an online learning perspective (Ahn et al., 2024; Ahn and Cutkosky, 2024; Nguyen, 2026; Xie et al., 2026). In addition, we highlight that our results are established under heavy-tailed noise, which not only generalizes the widely considered finite-variance condition but also more closely reflects practical settings.

## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we establish the first finite-time convergence rates and asymptotic convergence guar-antees for the original Adam algorithm in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. Our results provably apply to heavy-tailed noise with tail index p ∈ (4/3, 2] , even if the value of p is unknown. Moreover, many of our results are proved under the choice β1 = β2, aligning with the recent empirical findings of Orvieto and Gower (2025). Our analysis builds on the EO2NC framework developed by Zhang and Cutkosky (2024) and Ahn and Cutkosky (2024). However, our proofs require a more delicate and technical analysis than what is available in the existing literature. Several important questions remain open, which we list below. First, our analysis requires a randomly scaled learning rate for Adam , which is unfortunately not used in practice. It is unclear whether one can derandomize the learning rate and obtain a deterministic schedule while still ensuring convergence. Next, our finite-time rates, for both known and unknown p, are clearly suboptimal, since they lead to worse sample complexity than the best-known bound achieved by Clipped-Adam (Ahn and Cutkosky, 2024). Therefore, there is substantial room for improvement. Moreover, our results do not cover the regime where the tail index p ∈ (1 , 4/3]. Determining whether Adam , even with a randomly scaled learning rate, can converge in this regime remains an important problem, which we hope will be addressed in the future. Lastly, we note that our result also relies on a known time horizon T . Developing a result that does not require prior knowledge of T would make the result more practical.
