Abstract. A finite agent — a machine’s digital twin, or any bounded reasoner — sees a fixed, noisy world only through finite sensors, so its coherent output is not a point but a belief : a probability density over states (the Bayes posterior). Certainty is denied twice — by observation (Cramér–Rao) and by physics (Landauer) — both diverging at the boundary where the Fisher information blows up. We turn this finiteness into geometry: belief-cost geometry , the geometry of what it costs to change one’s mind. The cost metric is optimal transport in Wasserstein space, conformally reweighted by Fisher information (the price of the precision at stake), ˜ge,U = 2( e + U ) gW2 with relief U . It rests on two posed postulates: that revision cost is a scalar price on transport (the arena), and that the price is honest — one nat costs the same length everywhere (eikonal). Honesty selects the Fisher reweighting because transport demotes the Fisher information from the metric ruler of distinguishability (its role in Fisher–Rao information geometry) to the slope of entropy. Three results follow on the conformal class: a wall — a well-posed inference pushes certainty to infinite cost-distance once the relief dominates the Fisher information (necessity conjectured beyond power laws); an honest family — the eikonal price is equivalent to proportionality U = cJ ,the Fisher family; and a rigidity (essentially location-scale) — these geometries are hyperbolic and the Stam bound crowns the Gaussian as the most hyperbolic location-scale belief (ranking taken at e = 0 , where K is constant), the value −1/4 being one image of a relativity of cost . The cost of reaching a given precision then has a geometric floor diverging at certainty. Thermodynamics fixes the cost unit (one nat costs kBT at the wall) and motivates the framework; the results are geometric, in nats. 

Keywords. belief-cost geometry; optimal transport; Wasserstein space; Fisher information; infor-mation geometry; belief revision; eikonal equation; Stam inequality; Gaussian; thermodynamics of inference; entropy. 

# 1 Introduction 

A digital twin (Grieves and Vickers, 2017) maintains, inside a machine, an image of a real system — a territory, a city, a structure — in order to track or anticipate it. It is often conceived as a 

replica : a deterministic copy, regularly refreshed with data. In the setting we consider, however, the machine that realizes it is finite and accesses the system only through finite, noisy channels — whether a physical sensor or a finite agent (a human included), each itself noisy. Its coherent output is then a belief : a probability density over the possible states, produced by Bayesian inference, the coherent framework for reasoning under uncertainty (Cox, 1946; Jaynes, 2003; MacKay, 2003). Practice already does this — ensembles, data assimilation, Kalman filters (Kalman, 1960), often implicitly. Figure 1 shows this acquisition pipeline — a fixed hidden state, a finite noisy sensor, and the Bayes posterior it yields. Cramér–Rao inequality rules out (Cover and Thomas, 2006, Th. 11.10.1). Then by physical finiteness: erasing information dissipates energy, by the Landauer principle (Landauer, 1961). Both refusals diverge at the boundary where the Fisher information J blows up (Fisher, 1925). The belief is born of inference; finiteness only keeps it from freezing into certainty — a boundary no finite system crosses, which is also a reprieve . Lying at infinite cost-distance, the wall makes certainty not only unreachable but, by the symmetry of that distance, just as far in return: a Dirac sits infinitely far from every interior belief, so certainty — could it be held — would be beyond revision, dogmatic, beyond Bayesian repair by Cromwell’s rule (Lindley, 1991). A finite agent therefore lives in the interior, where every belief is a finite revision from any other — never frozen, never over-committed; keeping some uncertainty is what keeps it adaptable. The belief thus laid down, what remains to describe is geometric : the shape of the space where these beliefs live and move — its distances, its boundary (certainty, the excluded Dirac) and its curvature. A geometric toolbox already exists, and each piece, in its own way, meets that boundary: information geometry equips beliefs with the Fisher–Rao metric (Chentsov, 1972; Amari, 2016), under which the Dirac lies at infinite distance — but what it measures is distinguishability , not cost; optimal transport metrizes the displacement of mass (Otto, 2001), yet there the Dirac is at 

finite distance, with no wall; the thermodynamics of information prices the erasure of certainty (Landauer) and the dissipation of a driven belief (Sivak and Crooks, 2012; Aurell et al., 2011; Ito, 2023), but on a given geometry; and Bayesian mechanics ties a dissipative agent to a geometry of belief (Friston, 2019; Sakthivadivel, 2022), without a wall. To our knowledge, none re-metrizes beliefs into a geometry of revision cost in which certainty is an uncrossable boundary — a wall at infinite cost-distance — carrying a curvature; that is what we characterize. Where Fisher–Rao is the canonical geometry of distinguishability (Čencov), this is, we argue , its dual for revision cost —canonical within its class (theorem-grade on the power family; the general case is Conjecture C1, theorem 6.1), as Čencov’s is within his. This thesis organizes the paper (theorem 7.1).

3.1 Definitions and setting assumptions 

An agent has no access to the state of the world x, assumed fixed: a sensor interposes itself, and the agent receives only observations y. Bayesian inference answers this, through Bayes’ theorem (MacKay, 2003, p. 24). We adopt a static reading — a Bayesian posterior over the fixed state x

(MacKay, 2003, p. 460), not a sampling-theory confidence interval (MacKay, 2003, p. 464): the state is fixed, the agent has a finite number of observations, and we ask what it may, at a given instant, legitimately believe. We model the sensor as a finite-resolution, noisy channel, the transition matrix Qj|i = P (y =

bj | x = ai) giving the probability of observing bj when the world is in state ai, with forward map 

pY = Q p X (MacKay, 2003, p. 146): the agent sees a Q-filtered version of the world. The output of inference is then a belief , the posterior p(x | y) = p(y | x) p(x)/p (y) — posterior = likelihood ×

prior / evidence (MacKay, 2003, p. 29, also p. 50); inferring is the inversion of this known forward channel (inference, decoding and perception, one inverse problem). The belief is thus a density 

over the states of the world. To this belief certainty is denied twice , and both denials diverge at the boundary where the Fisher information J blows up. First through observational finiteness. Specifying the world to a resolution δ costs H(X) + ln (1 /δ ) nats, diverging as δ → 0 — the quantized differential entropy (Cover and Thomas, 2006, §8.3), whose discrete analogue is the essential bit content Hδ (MacKay, 2003, p. 75); the same barrier reads in estimation: by Cramér–Rao the variance of an unbiased estimator satisfies Var ( ˆθ) ≥ 1/J (θ), a zero variance would require J = ∞ (Cover and Thomas, 2006, Th. 11.10.1); and since the channel capacity is finite, processing creates no information, 

I(x; ˆx) ≤ I(x; y) (data processing, Cover and Thomas, 2006, Th. 2.8.1) ≤ N C (Cover and Thomas, 2006, Ch. 7). Then through physical finiteness: erasing information dissipates at least kBT ln 2

per bit (Landauer, 1961; Bennett, 1982), verified experimentally (Bérut et al., 2012), and the information capacity of a region of bounded size and energy is itself bounded (Bekenstein, 1981). Reaching certainty would require infinite energy and memory. The density comes from inference; the two forms of finiteness only exclude its contraction to a Dirac. figure 1 summarizes this chain, from the noisy world to the belief-density. The belief is the Bayes posterior p(x | y1: n) — the coherent epistemic state of a finite agent (Cox, 1946; Jaynes, 2003), not an arbitrary density. Bayes thus motivates the object (the coherent epistemic state) and the twofold refusal of certainty just stated; it enters none of the proofs below, which take only that the belief is a regular density (assumption 1). Bayesian updating is moreover 

multiplicative — a vertical reweighting of the density, p(x | y1: n+1 ) ∝ p(x | y1: n) p(yn+1 | x) —, whereas the cost geometry we build is a horizontal transport: the geometry prices a revision, it does not enact one. We study, at a fixed instant, the cost geometry of the posterior, not the dynamics of updating it. Finiteness thus settles the object — a density, certainty excluded — but leaves the geometry open. To turn the excluded certainty into distances, a wall and a curvature, we model what it costs to change a belief — the subject of the two postulates below (Section 3.2). What the frame then forces — the wall, then the honest family — is the subject of Section 4.

The Shannon–Khinchin axioms make this the natural reading once one elects to measure knowledge by an information measure at all — itself a framing choice: the measure is thus transparent and motivated rather than merely imposed, though, unlike the object forced by finiteness, the axioms (notably grouping) remain a posed normative choice (relax grouping → Rényi). 3.2 The two postulates 

What is costly to the agent is to change belief. To turn the excluded certainty into distances we make two posed, defended commitments — the arena (Postulate 0) and honesty (Postulate 1), nested. Each is a choice an informed reader could refuse, and each is defended; together they pin the cost geometry, while every other ingredient is forced, transparent, or borrowed.

Postulate 0 (the cost is a scalar price on optimal transport) . We model the cost of changing a belief as a scalar price field over the optimal transport of probability mass on the world’s state space, at fixed mass — a transport metric, reweighted by a scalar relief. Two clauses, both contestable: the cost is a transport (mass moved over the state space, not a reweighting in memory), and the price is scalar (isotropic — it depends on the belief, not on the direction of the revision). This is a statement about cost , not mechanism: a belief is updated by Bayes (a multiplicative reweighting), whereas the price of any revision is read as a transport length. The non-conformal (anisotropic) alternative is left open (theorem 6.1). From this commitment the arena follows rather than being posited. Mass conservation (a belief is a probability, total mass one), locality (the total cost is additive over a ground cost c0(x, y ) on elementary state displacements — the Kantorovich form ∫ c0 dπ, excluding coupling functionals non-additive in the plan (Santambrogio, 2015; Villani, 2009)) and the demand for a length structure single out an optimal-transport metric; a power ground cost d(x, y )p then pins the Wasserstein family Wp. One further commitment singles it out. 

Postulate 1 (entropic honesty) . The price is honest : one nat of knowledge costs the same length everywhere — the metric slope of knowledge −H is constant across the space of beliefs. This is a choice an agent could refuse (a distorted price would charge confidence unevenly); we adopt it as the calibration a digital twin owes its own uncertainty. by the Otto identity assumption 4, which lives on the arena). The two postulates are a ladder ,not two independent axioms — P0 builds the arena and its scalar price, P1 fixes that price. 4.4 Characterization II: eikonal ⇔ Fisher family 

This second equivalence is the characterization of Postulate 1 (Postulate 1): we make precise that demanding an honest price — the same length per nat at every point — selects exactly the reliefs proportional to Fisher information, the family F = {cJ }. The selection is possible only because of the demotion (principle 1): J enters as the slope of −H, not as the ambient ruler — so honesty imposes a real condition (in a distinguishability geometry, where J is the ruler, there would be nothing to select). It uses the scalar (conformal) form of Postulate 0 essentially : the constant-slope condition reduces to U ∝ J only because the reweighting is scalar; for an anisotropic deformation it would not separate (theorem 6.1). This is a uniqueness of family , not of object.

# 5 Consequences: a well-posed inference 

The wall (theorem 4.10) pushes the boundary of certainties ∂∞ to infinite de-distance: every curve approaching it has a cost length ℓe that diverges. Three consequences follow: a minimizer exists without collapsing, the cost bounds the entropy variation, and reaching a precision has a geometric cost floor.

Existence without uniqueness. Completeness and coercivity give the existence of a minimizer, not its uniqueness. The framework establishes well-posedness for existence and stability ; uniqueness remains open — the non-convexity of possible interaction terms, and the multiplicity of minima it may induce, are left open.

# 7 Related work 

Each building block is cited where it is used; we gather here the boundary between what is acquired and what is contributed, complementing the three positioning remarks (theorems 7.1 to 7.3). The contribution amounts to two moves: the eikonal characterization U = cJ (theorem 4.15) and the assembly into a cost metric with a wall; the rest is borrowed. 

Geometry of inference. Information geometry (Chentsov, 1972; Amari, 2016) equips beliefs with the Fisher–Rao metric, the only one invariant under statistical morphisms. Thermodynamics of information.
