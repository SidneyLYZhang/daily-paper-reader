RIEMANNIAN STRUCTURE AND OPTIMIZATION FOR A CLASS OF LOW-PARAMETRIC ORTHOGONAL MATRICES 

ALI ALIEV ∗ AND MAXIM RAKHUBA †

Abstract. In this paper, we are concerned with matrices formed by block-diagonal factors in-terleaved with fixed permutations – a flexible family of structured matrices. This class has recently drawn interest in deep learning architectures for its balanced expressivity-efficiency trade-off, yet efficient computational strategies for working with it remain to be found. We approach this prob-lem through Riemannian geometry and examine under what conditions this class admits a smooth manifold structure. For the practically important case of orthogonal two-factor matrices, we derive the essential Riemannian tools and propose efficient algorithms for their implementation. The algo-rithms leverage automatic differentiation, support parameter sharing within each factor, and avoid explicit dense matrix construction. We test them within the Riemannian optimization framework on the best matrix approximation problem and for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large language models. Beyond the two-factor setting, we study the geometric and matrix-theoretic properties of factorizations with a larger number of block-diagonal factors. 

Key words. Riemannian optimization, structured orthogonal matrices, matrix factorizations, Riemannian geometry, parameter sharing

1. Introduction. Structured matrix approximations are essential in numerical linear algebra and large-scale computing. They provide a practical way to balance an operator’s expressiveness with its computational cost. Classical structures, such as Toeplitz [12], circulant [7], and Cauchy matrices [24], allow for nearly linear matrix-vector multiplications. There are more recent examples, including butterfly matrix factorizations [20, 3] that have a rich history, originally arising from the structure of fast unitary transforms like the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) [25]. While well-established in numerical analysis, butterfly matrix factorizations have recently gained popularity in deep learning for model compression [5, 4]. A natural generalization of these butterfly-based and block-diagonal constructions is the Group-and-Shuffle (GS ) family [11]. This class unifies several previous approaches, such as Monarch matrices [4] and block-butterfly factorizations [5, 25], by parameterizing matrices as alternating sequences of block-diagonal factors and specific permutations. Euclidean optimization of GS -matrices ignores their geometry, which remains mainly unstudied despite empirical success (e.g., orthogonal fine-tuning [11]). In contrast, Riemannian optimization methods explicitly exploit the manifold geometry of the feasible set, offering a widely adopted framework for constrained problems [1]. This work utilizes a definition expressed in an alternative notation, introducing a minor formal distinction from the original work [11].

1.1. Contributions. This paper provides a geometric and algorithmic analysis of the GS matrix family. Our main contributions are: 1. We show by explicit counterexamples that unconstrained block factorizations with rectangular or square blocks need not form smooth manifolds. 2. For GS -matrices with orthogonal blocks, whose smooth manifold structure was recently established, we derive practical first-order Riemannian optimiza-tion algorithms. 3. We propose to use automatic differentiation (AD) to compute the tangent space projection while naturally handling overlapping block supports, without materializing dense matrices. As an application of this technique, we consider an efficient Riemannian Gradient Descent (RGD) algorithm and apply it to a matrix approximation problem and fine-tuning of LLMs. 4. We further extend our framework to support parameter sharing. This enables additional compression and speedup by adapting our efficient automatic dif-ferentiation mechanism. 5. We study geodesics for the case m = 2 under additional assumptions on the blocks, and for m = 3 we establish a connection with the CS decomposition. 6. We prove that Aorth 3 = A3 ∩ O( N ), and we discuss hierarchical and higher-order factorizations. 

1.2. Structure of the paper. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion 2 investigates the manifold properties of the GS family, presenting counterexam-ples for rectangular and general square blocks. Section 3 focuses on the orthogonal case: it derives the Riemannian geometry, details the retraction map in Section 3.4, and describes the tangent space projection in Section 3.1. Section 3.2 provides the efficient gradient computation via automatic differentiation, while Section 3.3 devel-ops the shared-parameter version of the projection and AD formulas. Finally, Sec-tion 4 presents numerical experiments that validate our framework. In the appendix, geodesics are studied under certain conditions on the blocks, along with the specific case m = 3 , for which interesting properties are revealed. Additionally, the invertible case is briefly discussed. 

Related Work. Structured matrices with subquadratic matrix-vector multiplica-tion costs (such as sparse, low-rank, and fast transforms) are classic topics in numerical linear algebra [14]. Butterfly matrices [25] were originally used to describe fast algo-rithms like the FFT. Recently, they have been used to replace dense layers in neural networks [27, 5]. Monarch matrices [4] combine block-diagonal factors with permu-RIEMANNIAN OPTIMIZATION FOR GS ORTHOGONAL MATRICES 3tations, and the GS class [11] generalizes these ideas by allowing flexible block sizes and varying numbers of factors. Optimization over constrained spaces, such as orthogonal groups, can be car-ried out using manifold optimization techniques [1]. Matrix manifolds help preserve gradient norms and control Lipschitz constants [19, 28]. Recently, orthogonal param-eterizations have been applied to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT). While additive low-rank updates (like LoRA [15]) are popular, multiplicative orthogonal updates (like OFT [26] and BOFT [21]) have shown better results in preserving the pretrained structure of weight matrices.

3. Riemannian Optimization Framework for Orthogonal Blocks. We de-velop an efficient Riemannian optimization framework for GS -orthogonal matrices, including retraction, tangent projection, automatic differentiation (AD), and param-eter sharing. Recall that for minimizing an objective function f on a smooth man-ifold M, the Riemannian gradient is grad f (X) = P TX M(∇f (X)) , where PTX M is RIEMANNIAN OPTIMIZATION FOR GS ORTHOGONAL MATRICES 5the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space. Parameter updates are performed using a retraction — a smooth map R : T M → M satisfying RX (0 X ) = X and 

DR X (0 X ) = id TX M (see [1, Definition 4.1.1]) — which ensures that the updated point remains on the manifold, e.g., Xk+1 = RXk (−τk grad f (Xk)) in the GD case. To avoid forming the full gradient, we use an AD trick: introducing an auxiliary func-tion on the tangent space and differentiating at zero yields the Riemannian gradient without dense matrix operations.

This leads to a black-box implementation compatible with automatic differentiation packages, thereby making Riemannian AD readily ap-plicable to deep learning tasks. 4. Experiments. To validate our theoretical results, we conducted several nu-merical experiments within our proposed training framework. For convenience, we implemented a PyTorch library that supports Riemannian gradient descent with the following features: various retractions (exponential, Cayley, polar) — all of which are valid retractions (see [1, Example 4.1.2]); overlap correction (if needed); as well as orthogonal blocks and complex-valued cases. The library is available at https: //github.com/alialiev78/torchgs. 

4.1. Synthetic tasks. First, we investigated model learning on synthetic tasks. We solve a GS Procrustes problem: find the nearest GS -orthogonal matrix to a given target, considering both the case where the target itself is a GS matrix and the case where it is an arbitrary (non-GS ) matrix. The baseline uses a Cayley parametriza-tion with Euclidean optimization (Adam/SGD), while our method uses Riemannian gradient descent on the manifold. For a fair comparison, each block in the baseline is constructed via the Cayley transform to ensure orthogonality, and the same grid is applied uniformly across all methods. The results for the GS -target case are presented in Figure 1, and for the arbitrary target case in Figure 2. Our method achieves comparable or better accuracy than the Cayley transform baseline. We also conducted experiments with parameter sharing, overlap, and the complex case. The results and conclusions are qualitatively similar; for brevity, we omit them here, but they are available in the file gs_testing.ipynb in our GitHub repository.

5. Conclusion. We have studied the geometric properties of GS matrices. For the case of orthogonal blocks, we present a robust framework for Riemannian opti-mization on GS -manifolds. We enable efficient gradient computations via automatic differentiation. The complexity analysis confirms that this structured approach in-curs limited overhead compared to standard training. Furthermore, we have obtained certain results for higher-order factorizations. Future work includes extending to higher-order optimizers to outperform AdamW, handling different block constraints, and a more detailed investigation of factorizations with more than two block-diagonal factors.

Appendix E. Generalization to Other Compact Matrix Groups. 

The Riemannian framework extends naturally to some other compact Lie groups with only minor modifications. For SO( b), the tangent space and gradient projection are identical to the orthogonal case O( b); the determinant +1 is preserved by sev-eral retractions provided the initial point lies in SO( b). For the unitary group U( b),the Riemannian metric uses the real part of the Frobenius inner product, and the orthogonal projection replaces skew-symmetric matrices with skew-Hermitian ones: 

skew( A) = 12 (A − A∗). Note that in U( b), an overlap is always present, the U( b)

case uses the same algorithmic structure; these modifications are straightforward and do not alter the method’s core efficiency or structural design. Mathematical and implementation details are available in the library’s source code.
