# Concentration of Regularized Sparse Random Matrices: Spectral Edge Bounds via Nonbacktracking Operators 

## Hai-Xiao Wang ∗

## September 23, 2026 

In sparse random matrices, spectral outliers (eigenvalues and singular values located away from the bulk) emerge due to degree fluctuations: high degrees inflate the operator norm, while low column degrees reduce the least singular value. As proved by Feige and Ofek [42] and Le, Levina, and Vershynin [62], degree regularization enforces concentration at the expected norm scale. However, precise bounds incorporating the cutoffs remain unexplored and challenging since regularization introduces dependencies among entries. For the first time in the literature, we provide variance and cutoff dependent bounds for extreme singular values and eigenvalues of regularized inhomogeneous random matrices. In the absence of regularization, our lower bound for the least singular value matches the same leading constant obtained by Brailovskaya and van Handel [24]. Moreover, our error term vanishes under the milder condition d/ log N → ∞ , as opposed to their stronger requirement d/ (log N )4 → ∞ . A key ingredient is to extend spectral radius bounds for nonbacktracking matrices to the dependent setting. We build on approaches for independent cases established by Benaych-Georges, Bordenave, and Knowles [15], as well as Dumitriu and Zhu [39], and carefully handle edges traversed only once. Our proof framework separates deterministic spectral comparisons from probabilistic estimates: once Loewner inequalities and columnwise variance controls are established, the remaining probabilistic analysis boils down to verifying the graph moment conditions formulated in this paper. We hope this framework can be extended to handle general random matrices with more complex dependencies. 

Keywords: Sparse random matrices, degree-regularized random graphs, singular values, eigenvalues, non-backtracking operator, moment method.

21. Introduction 

Sparse random matrices arise naturally in many applications, including community detection [1], numerical lin-ear algebra [71], and modern efficient attention mechanisms [34]. Over the past several decades, understanding the spectral behavior of sparse random matrices, especially the precise locations of the extreme eigenvalues, has attracted significant attention. As a motivating example, consider the Erdős–Rényi random graph 

G ∼ G(N, d/N ) on N vertices, where edges are sampled independently with probability d/N . Let A denote its adjacency matrix without self-loops, and assume d = o(N ). When d/ log( N ) → ∞ , the extreme eigenvalues of the centered adjacency matrix approach the semicircle edges; in particular, ∥A − EA∥ = (2 + oP(1)) √d [47, 113, 15]. However, when d/ log( N ) → 0, high-degree fluctuations cause ∥A − EA∥/√d → ∞ in probability, so the centered matrix does not concentrate at the √d scale [60, 62, 14]. In contrast, the removal of high degree vertices, a.k.a., regularization enforces the concentration of ∥(A − EA)I ∥ = O(√d) as proved in [42, 65, 62], where I represents the set of remaining indices supporting the new adjacency matrix. It is worth noting that even though the regularized matrix AI concentrates with the optimal rate √d, the precise constant factor multiplying √d has never been explicitly characterized. A related and equally important area in random matrix theory is the study of the least singular values, which plays a crucial role in understanding matrix invertibility [96] and conditioning [67], with broad implications in numerical linear algebra [71] and algorithms analysis [88]. In the context of rectangular sparse random matrices, we consider the bipartite Erdős–Rényi random graph ˜G ∼ G(V1, V2, d/ √mn ), where edges, containing exactly one vertex in V1 = [ n] and another vertex in V2 = [ m], are sampled independently with probability d/ √mn . Let ˜A ∈ { 0, 1}n×m denote the bi-adjacency matrix of ˜G with n ≥ m. We assume n ≥ m

and focus on the first m nontrivial singular values. As shown in [37], below a certain sparsity, the least of these fails to converge to the left edge of the limiting spectral distribution. This failure is due to the presence of low degree vertices, and in particular, the least singular value may actually become zero when isolated vertices emerge, i.e., zero columns 1 in ˜A. Consequently, it is natural to consider the effect of regularization on the smallest singular values. This raises the question of whether the removal of vertices with extreme degrees can likewise recover concentration at the left and right edge of the spectrum. To the best of our knowledge, the effect of regularization on singular values has not yet been studied. In this paper, we study spectral edges of mean-field type sparse random matrices after regularization with pre-determined thresholds. For the first time in the literature, we establish the explicit relationship between cutoff thresholds and extreme eigenvalues/singular values. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 1. Least singular value. When the lowest expected degree is at least log( N ) and the lower column cutoff is properly controlled, ensuring a non-vanishing fraction of columns remaining, we establish lower bounds for least singular values (Theorem 1.8), relying on both lower cutoff threshold and minimum column variance. When no thresholding is applied, the leading contant of our lower bound matches the one previously established by Brailovskaya and van Handel [24] for the regime d/ (log( N )) 4 → ∞ . In contrast, our error term vanishes when d/ log( N ) → ∞ , extending the regime where this tight lower bound remains valid. See Remark 1.9. 2. Largest singular values and eigenvalues. We provide upper bounds for the largest singular values (Theorem 1.6) and eigenvalues (Theorem 1.11), which depend on both the upper cutoffs and the maximal row and column variance profiles. Whereas estimating the left edge necessitates the lowest expected degree to be at least log( N ), upper bounds for the right edge remain valid as long as the highest expected degree exceeds some sufficiently large constant. 3. Spectral radii of nonbacktracking matrices. For nonbacktracking matrices constructed from matrices with dependent entries, we provide upper bounds for their spectral radii (Proposition 1.15) via the moment method (Lemma 3.14), extending previous analysis in [15] and [39] where independent entries are required. Our analysis show that traces for powers of nonbacktracking matrices are upper bounded, as long as the entries of the base matrix satisfy certain graph moment conditions in the Subsection 3.3, where independence is not mandatory. See Section 3 for details.

34. Loewner inequalities and edge bounds. We derive deterministic Loewner inequalities from the Ihara–Bass formulas, combining nonbacktracking radius bounds with entrywise and separate row and column variance bounds to control extreme eigenvalues and singular values. In particular, Proposition 2.5 provides a weighted Gram matrix comparison that sharpens the least singular value bound, and generalizes the previous established result in [37] for homogeneous entries. 5. Vertex retention guarantees. We quantify deletion cascades ( Remark 1.3) and retained dimensions, and establish near-full retention in the stated upper-cutoff and strong-band regimes (Section B). These guarantees ensure that the spectral bounds apply to submatrices of nearly the original dimensions, with explicit control on the proportion of vertices removed by regularization. 6. A general framework for spectral edges. We extend the framework that separates deterministic spectral comparisons from probabilistic input, in the vein of [15] and [39]. With the Loewner inequalities and the required entrywise and row/column variance bounds in place, the remaining probabilistic task (Section C) is to verify the graph moment assumptions listed in Subsection 3.3. We hope this framework can be adapted to a broader class of random matrix models, including those with dependent entries. Finally, we would like to make a remark on the effectiveness of the nonbacktracking operator method. As will be demonstrated at the beginning of Section 3, this approach is particularly well-suited for handling lower sparsity regimes, and sometimes it has been used in ways that sacraficed the accuracy to achieve near critical sparsity [39], perhaps unachievable by other methods. Here, we show that, when applied in full strength, the nonbacktracking operator method is not only capable of matching the accuracy of alternative approaches, but it can also be used to reach optimal sparsity thresholds.

1.5. Related literature. 

Edge universality for classical random matrices. For Wigner matrices, the work of Füredi and Komlós [47] and Bai and Yin [8] established the necessary and sufficient conditions for the convergence of largest eigenvalue to the semicircle edge. The distribution function of the largest eigenvalue was first identified by Tracy and Widom for Gaussian ensembles [100, 101], later extended to Wigner matrices [87, 84, 94]. A necessary and sufficient condition for Tracy-Widom law of Wigner matrices was established by Lee and Yin [64]. For rectangular matrices, Bai and Yin [118, 7] identify the convergence of largest and smallest singular values to the right and left edges of the Marcenko-Pastur distribution under finite fourth moment assumptions. Later, Tikhomirov [97] reduced the requirement for the convergence of smallest singular value to finite variance. Chafaï and Tikhomirov [27] extended the Bai-Yin to random matrices with dependent entries. Tracy-Widom fluctuation for the largest singular value was proved for isotropic [86, 78, 55, 79, 80] and anisotropic [41, 77, 12, 57, 63, 35, 116] sample covariance matrices, respectively. For the smallest singular value, Tracy-Widom fluctuation was identified in [43, 93, 79, 80]. See the recent survey [117] for more details. 

Spectral edges for sparse random matrices. For the Erdős–Rényi graph G(N, d/N ), it was shown in [47, 113] that the smallest and second-largest eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix converge to the edges of the support of the asymptotic eigenvalue distribution provided that d/ (log( N )) 4 → ∞ , and subsequently strengthened to d/ log( N ) → ∞ by Benaych-Georges, Bordenave, and Knowles [15] for the inhomogeneous case. When 

d/ log( N ) → 0, Krivelevich and Sudakov [60] showed that the largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix is asymptotically equivalent to the maximum of the square root of the largest degree and the maximal mean 9degree d, later refined by Benaych-Georges, Bordenave, and Knowles [14] for the k-th largest eigenvalue. At the critical scale d = b log( N ) for some constant b > 0, Alt, Ducatez, and Knowles [3], as well as Tikhomirov and Youssef [99], showed that spectral outliers beyond the semicircle edges, due to high-degree vertices, emerge if and only if b < 1/ ln(4 /e ). For the bipartite Erdős–Rényi graphs G(n, m, d/ √mn ), when d = b log( n +m) for some constant b > 0, Dumitriu, Wang, Wang, and Zhu [37] established the sharp thresholds the emergence of left and right outliers outside the Marcenko-Pastur edges induced by high- and low-degree vertices, respectively, with additional parameter restrictions for the lower edge. For inhomogeneous case, Dumitriu and Zhu [39] obtained bounds for largest and smallest singular values when d/ log( N ) → ∞ , while their lower bound is not as sharp as the current paper. Brailovskaya and van Handel [24] obtained sharp bounds for smallest singular value which is valid when d/ (log 4 N ) → ∞ . Che and Lopatto [29] proved that the distribution of the least singular value is the same as the distribution of the least singular value of a Gaussian matrix ensemble. See the recent reviews [117, 17] for more details. 

Concentration of regularized sparse random matrices and algorithm applications. Following the approach developed by Friedman, Kahn, and Szemerédi [46], Feige and Ofek [42] showed that removing high-degree vertices restores concentration. Later results extended regularized adjacency concentration to stochastic block models [31, 65] and hypergraph variants [38]. Through a different decomposition of directed inhomogeneous Erdős–Rényi graphs, Le, Levina, and Vershynin [62] established concentration for adjacency matrices and normalized graph Laplacians after regularization. For community detection tasks, these concentration results provide theoretical guarantees for regularized spectral clustering algorithms [28, 81, 56, 48, 50, 36]. 

Spectra of nonbacktracking matrices and algorithm applications. The Ihara–Bass formula [54, 13, 59, 5] relates the spectrum of the nonbacktracking matrix to the spectrum of the base matrix. Bordenave [16] reproved Friedman’s second eigenvalue Theorem for random d-regular graph using the nonbacktracking matrix. Wang and Wood [114] provided a precise description of the limiting empirical spectral distribution (ESD) for the non-backtracking matrices constructed from G(N, d/N ) when d/ log( N ) → ∞ . Benaych-Georges, Bordenave, and Knowles [15] obtained spectral radius bounds for nonbacktracking matrices of inhomogeneous sparse square matrices, with Dumitriu and Zhu [39] extending these results to the rectangular case. More recently, McKenzie [73] obtained a lower bound on the k-th largest modulus of an eigenvalue of the non-backtracking matrix for any fixed k. Spectrum of nonbacktracking matrices constructed from hypergraphs were also explored in [40, 92, 90, 44]. For applications, algorithms based on nonbacktracking matrices achieve weak recovery in Stochastic Block Models [72, 75, 74, 22, 89, 33, 91] and their hypergraph variants [92, 44]. Nonbacktracking algorithms are also applied to matrix recovery [21, 89] and tensor unfolding [90]. 

Non-asymptotic matrix concentration results. Rudelson and Vershynin [82] proved an optimal estimate of the smallest singular value of a random subgaussian matrix, combining the approach developed in [66] with certain Littlewood-Offord-type theorems [83]. Their results were extended to random matrices with weaker moment assumptions [58, 98], inhomogeneous entries [32, 69] and heavy-tailed entries [95, 49, 68]. Bandeira and van Handel [11] established nonasymptotic bounds on the spectral norm of random matrices with independent entries. Dimension-free bounds were later obtained in [52, 61]. Fluctuations at the Tracy-Widom scale were established in [23]. For heteroskedastic Wishart matrices, non-asymptotic concentration bounds were established in [119, 26]. 

Matrix concentration inequalities for sum of random matrices. Tropp established matrix concentration inequalities for sums of independent random self-adjoint matrices, providing noncommutative analogues of classical results through the matrix Laplace-transform method [109, 102]. Oliveira [76] and Tropp [106] established matrix Freedman inequalities for matrix-valued martingales. Moreover, Tropp [107] refined matrix Khintchine inequality by using matrix alignment parameter to reduce the dimensional dependence. More recently, Tropp obtained one-sided Gaussian comparisons for extreme eigenvalues [105, 104] and an exchangeable-counterpart proof of Brailovskaya and van Handel’s universality theorems [108]. Bandeira, Boedihardjo, and van Handel [9] established nonasymptotic upper bounds for the spectra of general Gaussian random matrices, relying on concepts such as asymptotic freeness [112] and strong convergence [51, 4] to account for matrix noncommutativity. Corresponding lower bounds for the spectral edges were subsequently established in [10]. For sum of random matrices with non-Gaussian entries, [24] proved concentration inequalities by comparing them to sums of independent Gaussian matrices having the same means and covariances. Exponential concentration inequalities for sums of dependent random matrices have been 10 established using an alternative exchangeable-pair method [70], while [110] demonstrates Gaussian universality for sums arising from ψ-mixing Markov chains. For noncommutative polynomial of random matrices, strong convergence, a.k.a, convergence of operator norm, were established in [18, 19, 20, 30]. See the recent reviews [17, 103, 53] for more details.

## 2. Proofs of Main Results 

Our framework separates the proof of spectral-edge bounds into a deterministic step and a probabilistic step. • Deterministic step : we establish the Ihara–Bass formulas to derive Loewner inequalities that control extreme eigenvalues and singular values in terms of spectral radius of corresponding nonbacktracking matrices, the maximum entry magnitude, and the row and column squared ℓ2-norms. • Probabilistic step : once the entrywise and row/column variance estimates are available, the spectral radius bounds for nonbacktracking matrices follow from trace-moment estimates and Markov’s inequality. In Section 3, those trace estimates are reduced to verify the graph moment assumptions in Subsection 3.3; we carry out this model-specific verification in Section C. This section is organized as follows: we first establish the deterministic comparisons in Subsection 2.1. Proofs of main Theorems are subsequently established in Subsections 2.2, 2.3 and 2.4, by applying these deterministic comparisons with Proposition 1.15. Before we proceed with detailed trace estimates, we first illustrate the advantages of using nonbacktracking matrices over the original sparse matrices. • Simpler walk enumeration. In Tr A2ℓ, a walk can repeatedly enter tree branches, retrace its steps, and explore other branches. These configurations contribute significantly to E Tr A2ℓ, but counting them sharply is difficult. In contrast, a nonbacktracking walk on a tree must follow a simple path without such repetitions. This leads to a more straightforward enumeration of path pairs in E Tr (Bℓ(Bℓ)∗).• Control of rare subgraphs. At bounded degree, rare subgraphs with several interacting cycles, such as tangles in random d-regular graphs [45], can dominate high expected moments. Nonbacktracking paths have enough structure to organize restrictions that exclude these walks and still agree with the original powers on a high-probability event. Bordenave [16] restricts nonbacktracking walks to traversed-edge supports with at most one cycle, obtaining a matrix that equals Bℓ with high probability for suitable 

ℓ ≍ log N . After removing the trivial mode, high moments of the derived weighted path matrices and the Ihara–Bass formula yield the sharp spectral bound. We note that merely replacing A by B does not automatically eliminate the rare-event obstruction. In this section, we extend the independent-entry arguments of [15, Section 5.1] and [39, Section 5] to dependent entries satisfying the graph moment conditions in Subsection 3.3. The additional difficulty is to control singleton contributions from edges traversed exactly once: these vanish for independent centered entries but may survive under dependence. We retain these words and combine the core enumeration with graph moment bounds that suppress long chains of singleton edges. Throughout the section, we fix an integer ℓ ≥ 3 and let d be the generic sparsity parameter, which are defined in (1.5) for the bipartite case and (1.18) for the Hermitian case, respectively.

Remark 3.15 (Genus summation and admissible powers) . ## 4. Future Directions 

We conclude this paper by discussing some future directions. 

Non-Hermitian sparse random matrices. Upper bound on spectral radius of non-Hermitian sparse random matrices with independent entries was established in [15] as well. It would be interesting to explore how thresholding affects the spectral radius in the non-Hermitian setting, similar to the results in Theorems 1.6 and 1.11 for Hermitian matrices. It would also be valuable to investigate how the trace estimates developed in Section 3 can be extended to the non-Hermitian setting. 

Hypergraph extensions. Let A ∈ R⊗ℓ be the adjacency tensor of a hypergraph H for some ℓ ≥ 3. The adjacency matrix A of H is formed by contracting A, so that A ij equals the number of hyperedges containing the vertex pair ( i, j ). Consequently, the entries of A are dependent in an essential way. In [6], it was demonstrated that the joint spectral distribution of these contracted tensor ensembles can be closely approximated by a semicircular family. The works [38, 36] established concentration results for regularized adjacency matrices of sparse hypergraphs, though without explicit sharp norm bounds. Since both the nonbacktracking matrix B

of H and the related Ihara–Bass formula have been developed in [92], it would be of interest to derive sharp norm bounds for these objects using techniques analogous to those in Theorems 1.6 and 1.11. 40 Acknowledgements
