# The Complexity of Finding Stationary Points in Nonsmooth Nonconvex Optimization 

## Guy Kornowski 

Abstract 

We prove that first-order algorithms require Ω( δ−1ϵ−3) gradient queries (in the worst case) to find a 

(δ, ϵ )-Goldstein stationary point of a Lipschitz function, at which there is a convex combination of gradi-ents within distance δ whose norm is at most ϵ. This lower bound is tight, matching known algorithms up to absolute constants, therefore resolving the complexity of convergence to stationarity in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. We further prove a tight lower bound of Ω( λ1/2ϵ−7/2) for finding points sat-isfying the recently proposed relaxed notion of (λ, ϵ )-stationarity, which allows combining further-away gradients. Our results reveal that convergence rates to nonsmooth stationarity are not affected by gradient stochasticity, in sharp contrast to smooth optimization. 

# 1 Introduction 

We consider optimization problems associated with an objective function f : Rd → R which is not necessar-ily smooth or convex. Such objectives are extremely common in modern applications, such as those involv-ing neural networks, but they cannot, in general, be globally minimized efficiently [Nemirovski and Yudin, 1983, Murty and Kabadi, 1987]. This discrepancy has motivated the introduction of local stationarity mea-sures for analyzing the convergence of optimization methods. For example, for smooth objectives where 

∇f is Lipschitz continuous, it is known that gradient descent can find an ϵ-stationary point, namely x such that ∥∇ f (x)∥ ≤ ϵ, within O(1 /ϵ 2) steps, independent of the dimension d. Moreover, this convergence rate is known to be optimal among all first-order methods [Carmon et al., 2020]. Extending stationarity-based convergence analyses to nonsmooth objectives is not so simple. Indeed, classical works only established asymptotic convergence in such settings [Burke et al., 2005, Kiwiel, 2007], and while it remains true that even simple subgradient methods asymptotically converge to stationary points under mild assumptions [Davis et al., 2020], getting anywhere near a point with small (sub-)gradient norm may require exp(Ω( d)) 

many steps [Kornowski and Shamir, 2022b]. In a landmark paper, [Zhang et al., 2020] bypassed such hardness results and introduced a relaxed stationarity notion based on the Goldstein subdifferential [Goldstein, 1977]. Roughly, a point x is called 

(δ, ϵ )-Goldstein stationary if there exists a convex combination of gradients in a δ-ball around x whose norm is less than ϵ. Remarkably, Zhang et al. [2020] showed that O(1 /δϵ 3) first-order queries suffice to find such a point, independently of the dimension d. Since then, Goldstein stationarity has found many applications and has been extensively studied, and there are by now many algorithms which are proven to converge efficiently to such points in various settings, including general Lipschitz optimization [Tian et al., 2022, Davis et al., 2022] and its variants such as stochastic [Cutkosky et al., 2023], zero-order [Lin et al., 2022, Chen et al., 2023, Kornowski and Shamir, 2024], constrained [Grimmer and Jia, 2024, Liu et al., 2024], private [Zhang et al., 2024, Kornowski et al., 2025], and minimax optimization [Shi and Luo, 2026], as well as higher-order extensions [Guan and So, 2026a,b]. A fundamental question throughout this vast body of work is understanding the complexity of find-ing (δ, ϵ )-Goldstein stationary points. For stochastic problems, Cutkosky et al. [2023] proved that Θ(1 /δϵ 3)

stochastic gradient queries are both sufficient and necessary, thus matching the smooth stochastic rate of 1arXiv:2609.17780v1 [math.OC] 15 Sep 2026 Θ(1 /ϵ 4) under the natural parameter regime δ ≈ ϵ [Ghadimi and Lan, 2013, Arjevani et al., 2023]. Interest-ingly though, for deterministic problems in which exact gradients are available, despite substantial effort, no algorithm is known to achieve a complexity better than O(1 /δϵ 3), attained already by [Zhang et al., 2020]. In comparison, as previously discussed, deterministic smooth optimization requires only O(1 /ϵ 2)

gradient queries. This left the following fundamental question open: 

What is the gradient-oracle complexity of finding (δ, ϵ )-Goldstein stationary points of nonsmooth noncon-vex functions? In particular, do deterministic gradients enable faster convergence than stochastic ones? 

In this work, we resolve this question by proving that Ω(1 /δϵ 3) gradient queries are necessary to find a 

(δ, ϵ )-Goldstein stationary point, even when the gradients are deterministic. In light of previously discussed algorithmic results, this lower bound is tight. Interestingly, our result implies that under the Goldstein framework, nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems do not suffer from stochasticity, thus answering an open question asked by Cutkosky et al. [2023]. In Table 1, we summarize the optimal (i.e., minimax) oracle complexities of stationarity in nonconvex optimization, with our result indicated in blue. In addition, we consider the complexity of finding (λ, ϵ )-stationary points, a stationarity notion pro-posed by Zhang and Cutkosky [2024], which relaxes Goldstein stationarity by allowing gradients to be combined beyond a ball (the formal definition appears in Section 2). This relaxation has proved to be widely useful in analyzing various algorithms which are popular in practice, such as Adam [Ahn and Cutkosky, 2024], schedule-free methods [Ahn et al., 2025], and spectral optimizers [Jiang et al., 2026, Li and Tsuchiya, 2026]. In this context, we prove a lower bound showing that Ω( λ1/2/ϵ 7/2) deterministic gradient queries are necessary to find a (λ, ϵ )-stationary point. This tightly matches previously known algorithmic upper bounds up to constants [Zhang and Cutkosky, 2024]. Algorithms. We consider iterative first-order algorithms, in the standard oracle complexity framework [Nemirovski and Yudin, 1983]: At each time step t ∈ N, an algorithm produces an iterate xt, receives the oracle response (f (xt), ∂f (xt)) , and chooses its next iterate, possibly at random, based on all the previous oracle responses. 1 In this work, we focus on the class of zero-respecting algorithms [Carmon et al., 2020], which is a general class defined as satisfying x0 = 0, and 

Pr [supp( xt) ⊆ ∪ t′<t supp( ∂f (xt′ ))] = 1 for all t ∈ N .

We note that this algorithm class subsumes the classical linear-span assumption [Nesterov, 2018], and in-cludes essentially all first-order algorithms of interest in our setting. 2 We further emphasize that this defini-tion allows algorithms to be randomized, which is crucial in this context, since it is known that deterministic algorithms cannot converge to Goldstein stationary points in a dimension-free manner [Jordan et al., 2023].

At a high level, the proof follows a standard lower bound recipe [Nemirovski and Yudin, 1983, Nesterov, 2018, Carmon et al., 2020, Arjevani et al., 2023]: construct a function f which simultaneously satisfies (i) a “zero-chain” property, meaning that the support of any subgradient at x is not much larger than the support of x, and (ii ) all solution points (in this case, Goldstein stationary) have large “progress”, meaning that their last coordinates are non-zero. Interestingly, the construction of our lower bound is directly informed by such upper bounds: We parti-tion coordinates into B = Ω(1 /δϵ ) “blocks” of length n = Ω(1 /ϵ 2) each, and construct a function with a “double-loop zero-chain” property (Lemma 4.2), ensuring both that each individual block cannot progress too quickly (i.e., an “inner chain”), and also that activating a block requires substantial progress in the pre-ceding block (i.e., an “outer chain”). The main technical challenge in the analysis is further guaranteeing that convex combinations of gradients, which can activate arbitrary blocks, remain large. To this end, for every point x with a zero final block, we explicitly construct a unit vector w′(x) ∈ Sd−1 whose inner product with all nearby gradients is Ω(1 /√n), implying that any convex combination of nearby gradients cannot be smaller than order 1/√n ≈ ϵ (Lemma 4.6). # 5 Discussion 

In this work, we proved tight lower bounds which establish the first-order complexity of finding Goldstein stationary points, as well as a related relaxation thereof. In particular, our results indicate that in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization, algorithms do not suffer from gradient stochasticity, thus answering an open question posed by Cutkosky et al. [2023]. It is interesting to note that in smooth nonconvex optimization, optimal convergence rates are in fact affected by gradient stochasticity ( ϵ−2 vs. ϵ−4). By contrast, in nonsmooth convex optimization, it is well-known that optimal complexities remain the same, since deterministic and stochastic subgradient methods converge similarly over convex functions (see e.g., Bubeck, 2015). Thus, it can be (very) informally argued that nonsmooth nonconvex optimization behaves more similarly to nonsmooth convex optimization, than to smooth nonconvex optimization. Finally, we note that while we established tight lower bounds for (possibly randomized) zero-respecting algorithms, which already covers algorithms of interest, our lower bound construction can be extended via known reductions to account for arbitrary randomized algorithms which use a local oracle [Carmon et al., 2020].
