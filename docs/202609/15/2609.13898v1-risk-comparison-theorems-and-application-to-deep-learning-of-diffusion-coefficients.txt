# Risk comparison theorems and application to deep learning of diffusion coefficients ∗

Arnaud Gloter 1 and Nakahiro Yoshida 2,3 1Laboratoire de Math´ ematiques et Mod´ elisation d’Evry, Universit´ e ´Evry Paris-Saclay †

2Graduate School of Mathematical Sciences, University of Tokyo ‡

3The Institute of Statistical Mathematics September 15, 2026 

Summary We investigate the nonparametric estimation of the diffusion matrix in stochastic differential equations featuring multidimensional, strong mixing covariate processes. We pro-pose a flexible statistical framework based on general function classes that does not require a linear basis representation, rendering our results directly applicable to deep neural network estimators. Our approach employs a two-step estimation procedure: constructing a preliminary nonparametric quasi-likelihood estimator and subsequently regularizing it via a β-H¨ older class approximation. We establish general risk comparison theorems between empirical and gener-alization risks for arbitrary estimators without relying on a specific probabilistic structure of the underlying process. In diffusion matrix learning based on n + 1 observations over the time interval [0 , T ], the derived upper bounds capture the intrinsic interplay between the T -rate, associated with the mixing behavior of the covariate process, and the intrinsic n-rate governing the volatility estimation. 

Keywords and phrases Nonparametric estimation, Diffusion matrix, High-frequency data, Deep neural networks, Generalization error, Risk comparison theorems, Strong mixing, Minimax op-timality.

This work concerns the nonparametric estimation of the diffusion coefficient in stochastic dif-ferential equations. We consider the model 

dY t = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)dw t,

where Y = ( Yt)t∈R+ and X = ( Xt)t∈R+ are multidimensional processes and w = ( wt)t∈R+ is a multidimensional Brownian motion. The process X is assumed to satisfy a strong mixing condition and represents a collection of covariates affecting the volatility of the process Y .These covariates may be endogenous or may include components of the process Y . In particular, this framework encompasses the classical diffusion setting for X = Y . We assume that high-frequency observations ( Xtj , Y tj )j=0 ,...,n are available, with hn → 0 and T ≡ Tn = nh n → ∞ .Our objective is to estimate the diffusion matrix S(x) = σσ ⋆(x) on a compact subset of the state space of X.In the parametric situation, the estimation of the diffusion coefficients from high-frequency observations has been extensively studied; see, for example, [12, 29, 40] for estimation over a finite time horizon, [22, 39, 42] for ergodic diffusion processes, [41, 43] for Bayesian analysis, [19, 28, 35] for jump diffusion processes, and [14, 15] for degenerate diffusion processes. The nonparametric estimation of S is addressed, when the process is observed over a fixed interval of time [0 , 1], in the references [2, 11, 17, 18, 20]. Since the diffusion coefficient can only be estimated in regions visited by the process X, these approaches heavily rely on the local time of the process. Consequently, they are inherently confined to one-dimensional diffusions. In this setting, the minimax rate of estimation is nβ/ (1+2 β), where β denotes the smoothness of 

S (see [17]). The estimation of the diffusion coefficient for ergodic diffusion processes observed over a long time horizon was studied by [5]. This framework has subsequently been extended to noisy observations in [33], and to diffusions with jumps in [34] and [1]. Recently, statistical inference based on repeated independent diffusion trajectories has at-tracted growing interest (see, e.g., [4, 13]). In this setting, the nonparametric estimation of the diffusion coefficient has been investigated by [6] and [9]. It should be stressed that, as far as volatility estimation is concerned, the underlying process is confined to the one-dimensional case in all of these references. In the present work, we aim to accommodate a more general structure for the process 

Z = ( X, Y ) and, in particular, consider multidimensional covariate processes. Moreover, our statistical analysis is formulated for general classes of approximation functions, rendering it readily applicable to deep neural network estimators of the diffusion coefficient. It is known that in high-dimensional contexts, a representation by neural networks tends to mitigate the curse of dimensionality in regression problems. It was shown in [32] that a ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) deep neural network can achieve a faster rate of convergence than wavelet estimators under structural constraints on the regression function. Approximation properties of neural networks and their statistical implications have been extensively studied in recent years; see, among others, [3, 24, 30, 37, 38]. A survey of recent developments in deep learning can be found in [7, 10, 36]. Theoretical guarantees for the generalization error of deep neural networks in the context of estimation of stochastic processes remain relatively limited. Some results for times series 2are established in [21, 23, 25] (see also reference therein). Nonparametric estimation of the drift function using deep neural networks has been considered for ergodic diffusion processes in [8, 27], while that for repeated diffusion processes has been investigated in [44]. Additionally, deep learning approaches for point processes have been explored in [16]. The ultimate goal of this study is to establish such a theoretical framework for estimating the diffusion matrix. Let Fn denote a class of candidate functions for the estimation of S. It should be stressed that the results of [5] contain a similar kind of constraint on the sampling step, taking the form of a condition between the dimension of the approximation space and √T .Standard techniques based on the large deviations of X over the time interval [0 , T ] only guarantee a generalization error rate of the form T −κ for some constant κ ∈ (0 , 1), because the 3mixing coefficient of the joint process ( X, Y ) is at least as large as that of X. However, this bound is suboptimal for the problem at hand. A key feature in estimating the function σ is the interplay between two distinct types of large deviations. One arises from the short-time movements of the Brownian motions, whereas the other is associated with the mixing covariate process X. Crucially, the temporally local fluctuations of Y carry more information for the estimation of σ than the long-term trend governed by X.Generally, large deviation estimates are utilized to control the covering number Nn of the model Fn. Thus, in our problem, the short-time Brownian large deviation plays a crucial role to derive the term n−1(log n) log Nn, which is essential for establishing the bound (1.1). This strategy is applied to the estimation of Rpn to derive (1.1), where Rpn denotes the empirical version of Rpn; that is, Rpn = E[Epn] with Epn given in (4.1). 1

Consequently, we can establish the bound (1.1) for Rpn provided that Rpn is dominated by Rpn up to a negligible term. Since Rpn and Rpn are characterized by ( Xtj−1 )j=1 ,...,n and (Xtj−1 )j=1 ,...,n , respectively, the large deviation associated with X (and its copy X) plays a primary role in this step. To this end, in Section 2, we establish certain risk comparison theorems to compare Rpn and Rpn solely through the large deviations of X over [0 , T ]. These risk comparison theorems generally assert that, regardless of the dependent models considered, the predictable generalization error is dominated by the predictable empirical error up to a residual term, which is controlled by the underlying process. The bound (1.1) for Rn also follows immediately from the estimate of Rpn.The organization of this paper is as follows. As noted above, Section 2 establishes compar-ison results between empirical and generalization risks for arbitrary estimators. This part is general and does not rely on any specific probabilistic structure, such as a diffusion process. Furthermore, we do not assume a linear basis representation for the estimator, making our results highly applicable to neural network estimators. Section 3 presents the model, estima-tion procedure, and statistical results. Section 4 provides the proofs for the upper bounds on generalization and empirical errors. Finally, Section 5 addresses the application to deep neu-ral network inference, presenting an upper bound in Section 5.1 and exploring the minimax optimality of the estimation procedure in Section 5.2.
