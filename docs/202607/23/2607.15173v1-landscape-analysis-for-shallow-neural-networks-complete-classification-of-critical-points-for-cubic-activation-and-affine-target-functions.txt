Abstract 

In this paper, we study the optimization landscape induced by the true loss for shal-low polynomial neural networks (PNNs) with h ∈ N neurons on the hidden layer, one-dimensional input and output layers, and a monomial activation of degree d ∈ N, trained against a non-constant affine linear target function. Our first main result provides for arbitrary activation degree d a sharp existence/non-existence criterion for global mini-mizers with necessary structural conditions. We show that the infimum of the loss is always zero and achievable with at least d active and visible hidden neurons – that is, hidden neurons with non-zero inner and outer weights – with pairwise distinct pivots. In contrast, if h < d , then the infimum cannot be attained and any minimizing sequence of parameters necessarily diverges to infinity. In the second main result, we provide a complete classification of all critical points of the loss function for the cubic activation. We show that the loss landscape admits no local maximizers , critical points cannot have exactly two distinct pivots, global minimizers require at least three distinct pivots, critical points with no active hidden neurons correspond to saddle points only, and consequently, 

non-global local minimizers and non-trivial saddle points arise only in networks where all pivots coincide. Moreover, non-global local minimizers require all hidden neurons to be active and visible with exactly one hidden neuron having a slope sign matching that of the target function. Our second main result also guarantees that each hidden neuron of a critical point that is not a global minimizer has either input-dependent or zero contribu-tion, but has no nonzero input-independent contribution, to its corresponding realization function.

# 1 Introduction 

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) have become a central tool in modern data-driven modeling, owing to their remarkable empirical performance in approximation, prediction, and representa-tion learning across a wide range of applications. From a mathematical perspective, a defining feature of these models is the nonlinear activation function applied at each hidden neuron. While this nonlinearity is crucial for learning complex representations, it fundamentally breaks the convexity of the underlying training problem, leading to highly intricate loss landscapes. Consequently, understanding how the choice of activation function interacts with network ar-chitecture to shape the geometry of the optimization landscape remains a fundamental problem at the interface of approximation theory and optimization. Although classical results [9, 18, 19, 32] dictate that feedforward networks with locally bo-unded and piecewise continuous activations are universal approximators if and only if the activation is non-polynomial, this limitation in expressivity is offset by the profound theoretical utility of monomial activations in optimization and specific learning tasks (cf., e.g., [1, 3, 13, 17, 26,36,48] and the references therein). While modern deep learning (DL) predominantly relies on piecewise-polynomial activations such as the rectified linear unit (ReLU), smooth activations 2such as monomials offer an analytically tractable setting where the loss function reduces to a polynomial in the network parameters. This algebraic structure enables a precise, global investigation of critical points and loss landscape degeneracies – a notoriously difficult task for non-smooth models. Polynomial neural networks (PNNs) – that is, ANNs with monomial activations – therefore serve as an ideal testbed for developing a rigorous understanding of optimization phenomena arising in the training of ANNs. The structure of the loss landscape is of fundamental importance for gradient-based opti-mization methods, which constitute the standard paradigm for training ANNs (see, e.g., [34,49] and the references therein). The convergence behavior of first-order methods such as gradient descent (GD) and its variants is governed by the geometry of critical points: global minima –the smallest possible loss value – correspond to exact representability of the target function in case of zero infimum, non-global local minima may obstruct convergence to optimal solutions (cf., e.g., [8, 20, 21, 24, 43, 47]), saddle points can substantially slow down optimization due to flat or nearly flat directions with small gradients (see, e.g., [5, 10, 30, 31, 40, 41] for avoiding convergence of GD type optimization methods to saddle points), and the presence or absence of local maxima reflects fundamental curvature properties of the objective; see, e.g., [12, 21, 24] for non-convergence and [2, 22, 23, 37–39] for convergence of GD type optimization methods to global minima depending, e.g., on the appearance of non-optimal critical points. A de-tailed structural characterization of critical points therefore provides a rigorous insight into the optimization geometry and clarifies how architectural parameters influence the existence and stability of suboptimal stationary points. Recently, there have been many results on the investigation of the geometry of loss land-scapes arising in empirical risk minimization for ANNs. For linear networks under squared loss, classical results [3, 26] show that every local minimum is global and all other critical points are saddles. Beyond the linear setting, [15] derived partial structural characterizations by ex-ploiting network hierarchy, without imposing restrictive assumptions on the target function or activation. For nonlinear activations, however, the landscape becomes more complex: [43] established the existence of spurious local minima for ReLU networks using computer-assisted arguments, while [17,36] showed that, for quadratic activation under squared loss, the geometry differs substantially from the linear case; nevertheless, non-global local minima may disappear under overparameterization (cf., e.g., [13,48]). For deep ANNs, non-global local minima vanish with high probability by increasing the width of the last hidden layer (cf., e.g., [35,44,45]). Ad-ditional theoretical studies of nonlinear landscapes include [6, 46], complemented by numerical investigations in [10], although these analyses rely on certain simplifying assumptions (cf. [7]). These results indicate that the landscape properties known for linear networks do not generally extend to nonlinear activations. While the preceding works focus on empirical risk, the population loss isolates the archi-tecture’s intrinsic optimization geometry from sampling effects, thereby providing a natural framework for theoretical analysis. Within this setting, [4] classified the critical points for shal-low ANNs with ReLU, leaky ReLU, and quadratic activations under squared L2 population loss and non-constant affine target functions, showing, in particular, that no non-global local minima occur for leaky ReLU and quadratic activations. In this work, we study the optimization landscape induced by the true squared L2 loss for shallow PNNs with a monomial activation of degree d ∈ N, trained against a non-constant affine linear target function. We show, in particular, that the loss function admits no local maximizers due to its structural properties and provide a sharp existence/non-existence criterion for global minimizers in terms of the relation between the activation degree and the network width. Moreover, in the case of cubic activation, we establish a full classification of all critical points in structural terms, describing global minimizers, non-global local minimizers, and saddle points via the activity patterns of neurons and their associated realization functions. 3In the following, we describe our main findings in detail in Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.2 below by employing in part the notations and terminologies used throughout the paper. The complete list of notations and terminologies employed in the paper is introduced later in detail in Subsection 1.4.

1.3 Structure of the article 

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Section 2, for an arbitrary activation degree d ∈ N and an arbitrary continuous target function f ∈ C(R, R), we exploit that the loss 7function L : Rd → R is a polynomial in the PNN parameter vector and derive in Lemma 2.2 and Lemma 2.3 explicit formulas for its gradient and the entries of its Hessian matrix. These results, in particular, show that the loss function is strictly convex with respect to the outer bias of an arbitrary input PNN parameter vector, implying the absence of local maximizers. In Section 3, for an arbitrary activation degree d ∈ N and a non-constant affine linear target function f ∈ P (1), we establish in Proposition 3.8 that the zero infimum of the loss function 

L is attained only in the wide regime – that is, when the network width h ∈ N satisfies h ≥ d

– whereas it cannot be attained in the narrow regime h < d , in which case every minimizing sequence necessarily diverges in the parameter space. We note again that Theorem 1.1 above is an immediate consequence of Proposition 3.8. In Section 4, we restrict to the cubic activation and a canonical setting with the identity target function f = id R and the unit integration domain [0 , 1] for the loss function L. Under this setting we determine in Lemma 4.8 all critical points of the loss function – zeros of the gradient ∇L – in terms of explicit expressions for the realization functions and the configuration of pivots of active hidden neurons. Finally, in Section 5, we combine the results from the previous sections to establish in Theorem 5.19 a complete classification of all critical points of the loss function for the cubic activation. We first characterize critical points in the canonical setting with the identity target function f = id R and the unit integration domain [0 , 1] for the loss function L. Then we employ the invariance property of the loss landscape under suitable affine transformations, established in Lemma 5.14, to extend these characterizations, in Proposition 5.16, Proposition 5.17, and Proposition 5.18, to the general setting with an arbitrary domain of integration ∅̸ = [ 𝒶 , 𝒷 ] ⊆ R

and an arbitrary non-constant affine linear target function f ∈ P (1). Theorem 5.19 is simply a collection of the results in Lemma 2.3, Proposition 5.16, Proposition 5.17, and Proposition 5.18. We note again that Theorem 1.2 above is an immediate consequence of Theorem 5.19. We remark that some of the symbolic computations in Sections 4 and 5 are verified using 

Wolfram Mathematica [50].

# 3 Existence and non-existence of global minima for mo-nomial activations 

In this section, for an arbitrary activation degree d ∈ N and non-constant affine linear tar-get functions, we establish in Proposition 3.8 in Subsection 3.5 below a sharp existence/non-existence criterion for global minimizers of the loss function in terms of the relation between the activation degree d and the number h ∈ N of hidden neurons of the PNNs under consider-ation. In particular, we show that the infimum of the loss function is always zero. Moreover, we prove that this infimum is achievable if and only if the network contains at least d active and visible hidden neurons with pairwise distinct pivots, which subsequently requires h ≥ d.In contrast, it can be seen, in Corollary 3.7 in Subsection 3.4 below which is the special case of Proposition 3.8, that if the width h of the hidden layer is strictly smaller than the activation degree d, then the infimum cannot be attained and any sequence of parameters along which the loss function tends to zero necessarily diverges. The proof of Proposition 3.8 relies on non-representability of polynomial target functions in the narrow regime ( h < d ) in Lemma 3.4 and a representability of polynomial target functions in the wide regime ( h ≥ d) in Lemma 3.5 in Subsection 3.2 below as well as Lemma 3.6 in Subsection 3.3 below where we construct a suitable sequence of parameters to show that the infimum of the loss function is zero. The proofs of Lemma 3.4 and Lemma 3.5, in turn, employ their respective sibling results concerning non-representability and representability of polynomials via shifted monomials, presented in Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 3.3, respectively, in Subsection 3.1 below. Finally, Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 3.3 make use of a well-known formula for the determinant of square Vandermonde matrices which we include, only for the convenience of the reader, in Lemma 3.1 in Subsection 3.1 below.

# 4 Determination of critical points for the cubic activa-tion 

In this section, for convenience, we consider in Setting 4.1 in Subsection 4.1 below a special case of Setting 2.1 in Subsection 2.1 above, where we restrict to the cubic monomial activation, the identity target function, and the unit interval as the domain of integration for the loss function L (corresponding to d = 3, 𝒶 = 0, 𝒷 = 1, and f = id R in Setting 2.1). Employing Lemma 2.2 in Subsection 2.2 above, we then determine all critical points of the loss function in 16 Lemma 4.8 in Subsection 4.4 below in terms of the number of distinct pivots of active hidden neurons and explicit realization functions. In particular, we show that only critical points with all hidden neurons being inactive or invisible semi-active can yield a constant realization function and at least two active hidden neurons with distinct pivots are required for the exact representation of the target function. We prove, in addition, that critical points with two distinct pivots of active hidden neurons must have, in total, at least three active and visible hidden neurons with pairwise distinct pivots. This guarantees the absence of critical points with exactly two distinct pivots of active hidden neurons. Consequently, we demonstrate that there are three different non-trivial realizations of critical points with all pivots of active hidden neurons coinciding. Lemma 4.8 is a collection of results presented in Lemma 4.2 in Subsection 4.2 below, Lemma 4.4 in Subsection 4.3 below, and Lemma 4.6 in Subsection 4.4 below, where we sep-arately investigate critical points with no active hidden neurons, with at least three distinct pivots of active hidden neurons, and with all pivots of active hidden neurons coinciding, re-spectively. In addition, only for the sake of completeness, we also include Corollary 4.5 in Subsection 4.3 below, which is a direct consequence of the latter results and explicitly shows that there are no critical points with exactly two distinct pivots of active hidden neurons.

# 5 Classification of critical points for the cubic activation 

In this section we recall our findings from the previous sections to establish in Theorem 5.19 in Subsection 5.6 below a complete classification of all critical points of the loss function for the cubic monomial activation and non-constant affine linear target functions. In particular, we first perform a characterization of critical points, determined in Section 4 above, in the situation of Setting 4.1 – where we consider the identity target function and the unit interval [0 , 1] as the domain of integration for the loss function. We then employ the invariance property of the loss landscape under suitable affine transformations, presented in Lemma 5.14 in Subsection 5.4 below, to easily extend the characterization results to the situation of Setting 2.1 with an arbitrary non-constant affine linear target function f : R → R and an arbitrary domain ∅̸ =[𝒶 , 𝒷 ] ⊆ R of integration for the loss function L : Rd → R.In Theorem 5.19, we recall from Section 2 that the loss function is a polynomial in the PNN parameter vector which admits no local maximizers, and we then show that the target function is exactly represented at global minimizers, which requires at least three active and visible hidden neurons with pairwise distinct pivots, that critical points with no active hidden neurons correspond to saddle points, and, consequently, that non-global local minimizers and non-trivial saddle points can appear only in networks for which all pivots of active hidden neurons coincide. Moreover, non-global local minimizers require all hidden neurons to be active and visible with exactly one hidden neuron having a slope sign matching that of the target function. Theorem 5.19 is simply a collection of all structural characterization results for critical points presented separately in Proposition 5.16, Proposition 5.17, and Proposition 5.18 in Sub-section 5.5 below. The structural characterization of 

• global minimizers, established in Proposition 5.16, is a direct consequence of Lemma 4.4 in Subsection 4.3 above, 

• saddle points, established in Proposition 5.17, uses Lemma 5.1, Lemma 5.2, and Corol-lary 5.5 in Subsection 5.1 below, and 

• non-global local minimizers, established in Proposition 5.18, uses Lemma 4.8 in Subsec-tion 4.4 above and Lemma 5.12 in Subsection 5.3 below. The proofs of Lemma 5.1, Lemma 5.2, and Corollary 5.5 are derived via the construction of suitable ascent and descent directions by partially involving the explicit Hessian formulas in Lemma 2.3 in Subsection 2.2 above. Our proof of Lemma 5.12 employs, for computational 21 simplicity, the reducibility of an arbitrary perturbation of a network with only active hidden neurons to effective parameters, established in Lemma 5.6 in Subsection 5.2 below, in order to guarantee the absence of descent directions.
