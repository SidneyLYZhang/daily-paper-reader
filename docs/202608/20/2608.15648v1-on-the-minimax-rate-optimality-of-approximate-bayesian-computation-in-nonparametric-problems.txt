# On the minimax-rate optimality of approximate Bayesian computation in nonparametric problems

Abstract 

Approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) replaces likelihood evaluation in conventional Bayesian computation by simulation and comparison of observed and synthetic data. We demonstrate that ABC can be minimax-rate optimal in nonparametric settings. Our main result is a general contraction theorem for ABC posteriors based on summary statistics sieves and a localized prior-mass condition. We apply this theorem to Gaussian sequence estimation over Sobolev ellipsoids and to density estimation over bounded Sobolev-type classes and, under model-specific conditions, construct ABC procedures whose ideal posteriors and posterior means attain the corresponding minimax rates. Conditional on sampling from the specified priors, we show that the conventional Monte Carlo rejection ABC algorithm inherits the same rates when the number of simulation proposals grows at a sufficiently large exponential rate in the effective dimension. 

Keywords. Approximate Bayesian computation; posterior contraction; minimax risk; Gaussian sequence model; density estimation. 

# 1 Introduction 

1.1 Approximate Bayesian computation 

Approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) is a likelihood-free approach for Bayesian inference in models for which simulating data is feasible but evaluating the likelihood is impossible or computationally prohibitive. In its basic and most common rejection form, one draws a parameter θ

from a prior measure, simulates synthetic data Z from the model defined by θ, computes a discrepancy between Z and the observed data X, and retains the parameter value θ if the discrepancy is below a predetermined tolerance. The method has its origins in population genetics and coalescent models (Tavare et al., 1997; Pritchard et al., 1999), with regression-adjusted ABC introduced by Beaumont et al. (2002). Broader accounts of rejection, importance sampling, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), sequential Monte Carlo (SMC), regression-adjusted, synthetic-likelihood, and discrepancy-based variants are given by Marin et al. (2012), Karabatsos and Leisen (2018), and Sisson et al. (2018). In the typical summary-statistic construction, a measurable map Sn : Xn → S n sends a dataset in the sample space Xn to a summary-statistic space Sn equipped with a discrepancy ρn. The observed 1arXiv:2608.15648v1 [math.ST] 16 Aug 2026 statistic Sn(X) is compared to the synthetic statistic Sn(Z). 1.2 Nonparametric problems and minimax rates 

We consider the application of ABC approaches to nonparametric statistics, and focus on a pair of benchmark problems. The first is Gaussian sequence estimation over a Sobolev ellipsoid. The observation is 

Xj = θ0j + n−1/2ξj , j ≥ 1,

where the parameter sequence θ0 = ( θ01 , θ 02 , . . . ) belongs to a Sobolev ellipsoid in ℓ2. This is a standard sequence-space smoothing problem; see Ibragimov and Has’minskii (1981, Chapter VII, Sections 4 and 5), Nussbaum (1999), and Johnstone (2017, Chapters 3 and 5). The second problem is density estimation over bounded Sobolev-type density classes on [0 , 1] d, expressed through the coefficients of a series expansion. Multivariate smoothness classes, density-estimation lower bounds, and associated arguments are developed in Klemela (2009, Chapters 10 and 11); related minimax lower-bound tools are treated in Gine and Nickl (2016, Section 6.3). These benchmark experiments are used to isolate the statistical effect of the ABC approximation and to compare it directly with established minimax theory, rather than because their likelihoods are themselves unavailable. In both examples, α denotes the smoothness index. In the Gaussian sequence problem, the relevant squared-risk rate is n−2α/ (2 α+1) in ℓ2 loss; see Ibragimov and Has’minskii (1981, Chapter VII, Section 5) and Nussbaum (1999). For the nondegenerate density classes considered below, under an Assouad–hypercube condition, the squared L2 rate is n−2α/ (2 α+d); see, for example, Klemela (2009, Corollary 11.11). 

1.3 Main contribution 

The paper makes three contributions. First, it develops a general route for proving convergence rates for ABC posteriors built from growing summary statistics sieves. The central theorem separates the statistical problem from the sampling algorithm: it asks whether the ideal ABC posterior assigns enough mass near the truth and sufficiently little mass to well-separated parameters when 2the summary dimension Kn grows with n. The required local prior mass is bounded below by 

exp {− CK n} on ϵn-neighborhoods, where ϵn is the target minimax-optimal contraction radius. This is the same exponential small-ball argument that appears in standard nonparametric posterior-contraction arguments; compare Ghosal and van der Vaart (2017, Chapter 8) and Castillo (2024, Chapters 1–3). Second, the abstract result is applied to Gaussian sequence estimation over Sobolev ellipsoids. The construction is a smoothness-matched finite Gaussian series distribution with independent coordinates at the effective dimension Kn ≍ n1/(2 α+1) , and the prior is obtained by conditioning this law on a slightly enlarged ellipsoid. This gives a prior for which the ideal ABC posterior and its posterior mean attain the classical minimax rate. Third, the same proof mechanism is applied to density estimation over bounded Sobolev-type density classes. The density result uses finite-dimensional coefficient summaries and a smoothness-matched constrained Gaussian series prior on densities. The assumptions state explicitly the two analytic requirements needed in this setting: the finite coefficient projection must approximate the target density at the bias scale, and empirical coefficient summaries must concentrate at the variance scale. Under these requirements, and under an Assouad–hypercube condition, ABC again attains the nonparametric minimax rate. 

1.4 Relation to the ABC literature 

Our contributions relate to three directions of the ABC literature. The first direction, ABC asymptotic theory, studies posterior concentration, limiting shape, and Monte Carlo efficiency in fixed-dimensional settings. Frazier et al. (2018) give general concentration and limiting-shape results for ABC posteriors based on summary statistics. Li and Fearnhead (2018a) show that regression adjustment can recover calibrated uncertainty under suitable bandwidth choices, and Li and Fearnhead (2018b) analyze the corresponding Monte Carlo efficiency. Appendix E is included to connect the present minimax formulation with this literature. The second direction studies full-data or discrepancy-based ABC. Bernton et al. (2019) propose Wasserstein ABC and establish consistency and concentration properties in general settings. Jiang et al. (2018) use a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence estimator as a data discrepancy and show asymptotic consistency of the induced quasi-posterior. Nguyen et al. (2020) study energy-statistic ABC in an importance-sampling framework. Nguyen et al. (2025) studies ABC posterior concentration under weak assumptions on the discrepancies including summary statistics-based methods. Forbes et al. (2022) construct surrogate posterior summaries and compare them by distributional metrics. Legramanti et al. (2025) introduce Rademacher complexity into discrepancy-based ABC theory, giving unified uniform concentration bounds for integral probability semimetrics such as maximum mean discrepancy and Wasserstein distance. The present paper differs by focusing specifically on the behavior of ABC posterior measures for classical nonparametric minimax problems rather than establishing more general results. The third direction concerns high-dimensional ABC and the curse of dimensionality. Blum (2010), Barber et al. (2015), and Nott et al. (2018) show that kernel or rejection ABC deteriorates rapidly as the dimension of the summary statistic grows. In the nonparametric problems that we study, Kn ≍ nϵ 2 

> n

grows with n, and the normalizer lower bound yields the sufficient rejection-sampling condition Nn exp {− κK n} → ∞ . Conditional on exact sampling from the specified priors, the transfer argument therefore guarantees the minimax rate when the number of prior–simulator proposals is exponential in the effective dimension; it does not establish that this number is necessary. Computationally targeted alternatives include MCMC-ABC and SMC-ABC (Marin et al., 2012; Sisson et al., 2018), regression-adjusted ABC (Beaumont et al., 2002; Li and Fearnhead, 2018a), 3high-dimensional ABC methods (Nott et al., 2018), and synthetic-likelihood methods (Frazier et al., 2023). Such computational analysis is kept separate here from the statistical minimax problem.

# 6 Discussion 

The preceding results show that ABC can be statistically minimax-rate optimal in the two nonpara-metric problems studied here when the summary statistics, tolerance, and prior are calibrated to the bias–variance tradeoff of the problem and the stated Assouad–hypercube condition holds for the density-estimation lower bound. The proof identifies a simple statistical mechanism behind this conclusion. For the prior component of this mechanism, it is enough that an n-dependent finite series prior, constructed from a coefficient law with a locally positive continuous density and suitable support constraints, assigns at least exp {− CK n} mass to ϵn-neighborhoods of the target and controls the coordinates not seen by the ABC summary statistic. The typical-data, local-acceptance, and separation conditions stated in Condition 3.1 are required in addition. In the Gaussian sequence problem, the prior is an explicit truncated Gaussian series prior. Its role is to provide enough local prior mass at the minimax scale while retaining a finite-dimensional computational representation. More general finite random-series priors are studied by Shen and Ghosal (2015). In density estimation, the additive constrained Gaussian series prior is proper under the projection-feasibility condition stated in Section 5; it is a finite-dimensional random-series construction with the density constraints imposed directly on the additive expansion. Positive-link density priors enforce positivity by exponentiating and normalizing a series expansion, as in log-spline 16 and infinite-dimensional exponential-family constructions (Ghosal et al., 2003; Scricciolo, 2006), but they make the map from prior coefficients to density coefficients nonlinear. A minimax-rate ABC theorem for those priors would therefore require a separate proof of the same local prior-mass and separation conditions for the induced density-coefficient summaries. The results also clarify a computational distinction. Under the fixed-dimensional assumptions of Appendix E, with dθ denoting the parameter dimension, the prior-rejection scheme inherits the root-n

statistical rate under the polynomial sufficient condition Nnn−dθ /2 → ∞ . In the nonparametric problems studied here, the effective summary-statistic dimension Kn diverges, and, conditional on exact prior sampling, the uniform transfer bound gives an exponential sufficient number of proposals for rejection sampling from the prior. These are sufficient guarantees obtained from denominator lower bounds; they are not lower bounds on the required number of proposals or on end-to-end computational cost. Targeted alternatives include SMC-ABC and MCMC-ABC algorithms described in Marin et al. (2012) and Sisson et al. (2018), regression-adjusted ABC introduced by Beaumont et al. (2002) and analyzed by Li and Fearnhead (2018a), high-dimensional ABC methods surveyed by Nott et al. (2018), and synthetic-likelihood methods analyzed by Frazier et al. (2023). The computational analyses of such methods require bespoke arguments and are not addressed here. Several extensions are natural. A first direction is to determine which parts of the local-mass proof transfer to kernel-weighted or importance-sampling ABC posteriors, where the hard acceptance event is replaced by a continuous weight. In that setting the denominator argument would become a lower bound on an averaged kernel weight near the target, and the separated-numerator argument would require upper bounds on the kernel tail rather than on a rejection probability; see Karabatsos and Leisen (2018) and Nguyen et al. (2020) for related importance-sampling and discrepancy-weighted ABC formulations. A second direction is to develop minimax-rate theory for ABC based on full-data discrepancies, such as Wasserstein, energy, maximum mean discrepancy, or KL-type discrepancies. Existing discrepancy-based ABC theory gives concentration and limiting-posterior results for several such choices (Bernton et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2018; Nguyen et al., 2020, 2025; Legramanti et al., 2025). The minimax problem requires more model-specific information: which discrepancy, tolerance, and prior resolution reproduce the nonparametric rates, and which parts of the denominator, separation, and Monte Carlo transfer program require new technical input.
