Abstract 

Weak-to-strong generalization is the phenomenon where a strong student model trained with labels produced by a weak teacher model is able to generalize better than the teacher. In this pa-per, we study this phenomenon in two-layer random feature networks where the model strength is determined by its width. Using tools from random matrix theory, we derive deterministic equiva-lents for the population errors of an optimally trained teacher and a student trained with gradient flow. For ReLU activation and a pure spherical harmonic target, we obtain sharp asymptotics under a Gaussian universality assumption, showing a quadratic improvement: the student error scales as the square of the teacher error. These results attain the general lower bound of Medvedev et al. [2025]. We also analyze how the student behaves under more general stopping times and targets supported on multiple harmonic degrees, characterizing the regimes in which weak-to-strong generalization occurs and identifying the transition between quadratic, non-quadratic, and no improvement. 

# 1 Introduction 

Can a student trained only on a teacher’s predictions surpass the teacher? A small model trained on the true labels still makes errors because of its limited capacity, and a larger student that sees only these flawed labels might be expected to reproduce them. Weak-to-strong generalization (WTS), introduced in Burns et al. [2024], is the phenomenon where it does not: the strong student generalizes better than the weak teacher that supervises it. We study this phenomenon theoretically in a random feature neural network, in a regression problem with squared loss, where the strength of a model is its capacity, that is, its number of neurons. We choose this model for three reasons. Its strength has a single unambiguous meaning, the width. Its population loss is governed by the spectrum of the feature kernel, so the teacher’s error and the student’s training dynamics can be written explicitly, and, for data on the sphere, the expansion of the target in spherical harmonics gives a natural notion of its complexity, its harmonic degree. Finally, random feature models, introduced in Rahimi and Recht [2007] as a way of reducing computation in kernel methods, are conceptually simple yet exhibit phenomena also present in deep networks, and are a standard surrogate for them [Mei and Montanari, 2022, Bombari and Mondelli, 2024, Dohmatob and Bietti, 2022, Chizat et al., 2019]. Our aim is to show that WTS emerges already in this model, and to quantify the contribution of each of its components. We closely follow the setup pioneered in Medvedev et al. [2025], where random feature networks also represent the student and teacher. Our two-step training procedure is the same as theirs: we first optimally train the teacher over the population error, so that its only source of error is its capacity, and then train the student, initialized at zero, on the teacher’s labels using early-stopped gradient flow. The goal is to stop the training before the student imitates too many of the teacher’s mistakes, which is an implicit regularization effect [Li et al., 2020, Yao et al., 2007, Raskutti et al., 2014, Ali et al., 1arXiv:2610.09044v1 [stat.ML] 6 Oct 2026 2019, Heckel and Yilmaz, 2021]. In our specific case, it can be understood as a spectral cutoff: the student imitates the teacher’s components up to the target degree, while the remaining components barely move from their initialization. We differ from Medvedev et al. [2025] both in method and in scope. In method, we use random matrix theory, specifically the theory of Dyson equations and deterministic equivalents [Erd˝ os, 2019]. A deterministic equivalent removes the randomness of the features from the population errors and gives explicit formulas for both student and teacher errors: a single-resolvent formula for the teacher and a two-resolvent formula for the student, the latter being a deterministic equivalent for a two-stage training pipeline. This simplified mechanism reproduces the errors accurately and makes their analysis straightforward. In scope, the formulas hold at every stopping time and for general targets, which lets us analyze the optimal improvement and the full effect of the stopping time. We leave open the comparison between the deterministic equivalents and the random feature re-gression itself. Results of this kind exist in the single-stage setting [Defilippis et al., 2024, Cheng and Montanari, 2024, Misiakiewicz and Saeed, 2024]; extending them to the two-stage pipeline is a matter of mathematical technicalities that we do not expect to reveal new information about the learning problem, and our numerical comparisons show that the deterministic equivalents match the gradient-flow simulations closely. 

Our contributions. 

• Deterministic equivalents and a general lower bound. We derive deterministic equiva-lents for the population errors of the optimally trained teacher and of the early-stopped student (Section 3, Appendix B). The student error splits into a squared bias and a variance term. The decomposition explains both the improvement over the teacher and its loss under prolonged training, and implies that the student error is at least the square of the teacher error, for any activation and any target (Theorem 1). 

• Quadratic improvement. For ReLU activation on the sphere and a pure spherical-harmonic target, we show that an appropriate stopping time attains this quadratic improvement (Theo-rem 2, Section 4), attaining the lower bound of Theorem 1. The ReLU statements are conditional on a Gaussian universality assumption (Assumption 1). 

• Pure harmonic targets and general stopping times. We characterize the phase diagram of the student error in terms of the teacher’s capacity exponent α (m = dα neurons) and the stopping time exponent γ (T = Θ( dγ+1 )), identifying which of the two error terms controls the error in each region and the transitions between quadratic, nonquadratic, and no improvement (Theorem 4, Section 5). 

• Mixed targets and general stopping times. For targets with components on several har-monic degrees, we give a condition for WTS in terms of unlearned target energy, and a coefficient-independent guarantee of polynomial improvement when the teacher can learn every mode. Two examples show that the improvement can nevertheless fail to be quadratic, because different modes require incompatible stopping times (Section 6). 

Related work. Since the first empirical investigation of Burns et al. [2024], many works have con-sidered a theoretical analysis using simple settings. Some focus on how data structure influences the phenomenon. Lang et al. [2024] study the student’s correction of teacher mistakes and its performance on regions not covered by teacher labels. Shin et al. [2025] express improvement through the frequency of points containing both easy and hard patterns, while Wu and Sahai [2025] study a transition from random guessing to perfect generalization in classification. Other works quantify the improvement through the misfit error , which measures the disagreement between the student and teacher [Charikar et al., 2024, Yao et al., 2025, Mulgund and Pabbaraju, 2025]. From the point of view of representations, Xue et al. [2025] identify directions poorly captured by the 2teacher but well represented by the student. Dong et al. [2025] analyze how the student inherits teacher variance along shared directions and reduces it along others. In these works, differences between the two representation spaces prevent the student from fully inheriting the teacher’s noise, which is the source of the improvement. With respect to regularization, Moniri and Hassani [2026] show that a student in linear ridge regression can surpass the teacher through better regularization, especially when it is aligned with the target. This is related to Medvedev et al. [2025] and our work, where regularization is imposed implicitly by early-stopped gradient flow. Beyond linear settings, Oh et al. [2025] use convolutional networks and also find that early stopping improves student performance in a data-abundant regime. Closest to our setting are analyses of weak-to-strong generalization in random feature and high-dimensional linear regression, where teacher and student differ in width or in regularization and the labels are produced by a fitted teacher. Ildiz et al. [2025] analyze ridge regression trained on the predictions of a surrogate model in the proportional regime, characterizing when the student improves on the teacher and deriving scaling laws, and Wu et al. [2026] show that weak-to-strong training improves the scaling law of random feature ridge regression. In both works, as in ours, deterministic equivalents play a central role. Appendix A gives an extended discussion.

# 7 Conclusions and further discussion 

Using deterministic equivalents, we have computed the leading-order population errors of a random feature teacher and an early-stopped student, and shown that a pure harmonic target admits optimal quadratic improvement. The mechanism is early stopping: the student learns each spectral component of the target on its own time scale and inherits the teacher’s feature variance only on the slower time scale of the teacher’s capacity edge, and weak-to-strong generalization is the window between the two. One of the key takeaways is that this window is target dependent: different targets require different stopping times for optimal improvement, and for a target with several components, incompatible stopping times can rule out quadratic improvement even when every component would admit it on its own. Nonetheless, the effect is robust provided the teacher has the capacity to learn every component of the target: a fixed gap below capacity guarantees polynomial improvement for a range of stopping times. When the target has a component beyond the teacher’s capacity, no stopping time gives more than a constant-factor gain. This illustrates a key limitation of weak-to-strong generalization: the student can suppress the teacher’s variance, but it cannot recover what the teacher could not represent. We mention some key limitations of our analysis. The conclusions remain conditional on Gaussian universality and deterministic-equivalent accuracy; controlling that approximation is a mathematical direction for further work. We have taken the student infinitely wide. A finite student of width MST 

adds a second source of feature variance, and one expects a threshold effect: once MST is large enough relative to the teacher. Finite student widths admit deterministic equivalent analaysis as well.

# A Extended related work 

Since Burns et al. [2024] first empirical investigation of the WTS phenomenon, many works at-tempted to do a theoretical analysis of it using simple settings. We discuss below a selection of works representing the range of different approaches used to understand the phenomenon. 

Data-dependent mechanisms. First, some works also focus on understanding how data struc-ture influences the WTS phenomenon. Lang et al. [2024] demonstrate that the gain in performance comes not only from the student’s correction of the teacher’s mistakes but also from the student’s good performance on a portion not covered by the teacher labels. This occurs when one assumes that the strong model’s predictions are robust in neighborhoods of pseudo-labeled points (teacher-labeled points) and that bad points, that is, points with incorrect pseudo-labels, have many good neighbors. They also provide empirical evidence for these assumptions in finite samples. Shin et al. [2025] define a quantity called overlap density , which represents how frequently a dataset contains points with both hard and easy patterns. The weak model learns only the easy patterns, but the strong model is able to learn the hard patterns from the overlap points and thereby generalize to points with only hard patterns. Building on this intuition, they theoretically prove that the WTS phenomenon can be ex-pressed as a function of the overlap density. In the classification setting, Wu and Sahai [2025] define weak and strong models based on how well their features represent the data features. Considering a teacher whose labels are asymptotically equivalent to random guessing, they show that, under certain 15 conditions, the student’s performance undergoes an asymptotic transition from random guessing to perfect generalization. 

Misfit error. Charikar et al. [2024] consider a more general notion of weak and strong models, in which the difference between them is determined by how well their representations capture the underlying data. This can include differences in expressivity, as in our setting, as well as other factors such as the amount of pretraining. They study a regression setting with the squared loss and quantify the WTS gain in terms of the misfit error . This quantity is defined as the error of the strong student model on the labels generated by the weak teacher model; equivalently, it measures the squared disagreement between the student and the teacher. Under their assumptions, they show that the error of the strong model evaluated on the ground-truth labels is bounded above by the error of the weak model on the ground-truth labels minus the misfit error. In our notation, this corresponds to LST ≤ L TE − misfit Using representation and projection techniques, they show that, globally, the component of the teacher predictor that is not reproduced by the student contributes to the teacher’s error relative to the ground-truth labels. Consequently, the larger the misfit, the larger the improvement. Yao et al. [2025] establish a corresponding upper bound while also studying lower bounds. Unlike Charikar et al. [2024], they use a KL-divergence-based loss instead of the squared loss. Other work that studies the misfit error includes Mulgund and Pabbaraju [2025], who extend the misfit-based inequality to loss functions given by Bregman divergences. This extension also includes the classification setting, since cross-entropy can be expressed in terms of the KL divergence, which is a Bregman divergence. 

Representation geometry. Other works study WTS from a geometric and representation-based perspective. Xue et al. [2025] quantify the WTS improvement in terms of the overlap between the stu-dent principal-component subspace and the orthogonal complement of the teacher principal-component subspace. The principal-component subspace of each model contains the dominant directions of vari-ance in its internal representation. Therefore, this overlap identifies directions that are not captured by the teacher but are well represented by the student, allowing the student to correct some of the teacher’s errors. From a related geometric perspective, Dong et al. [2025] analyze ridgeless regression with intrinsically low-dimensional feature spaces. They show that the student inherits the teacher’s variance along directions shared by their representations. In contrast, along directions represented by the teacher but not by the student, this variance is reduced by a factor given by the ratio between the dimension of the student feature space and the number of weakly labeled samples. Thus, differences between the two representation spaces prevent the student from fully inheriting the teacher noise and then improving generalization. 

Regularization and early-stopping. With respect to how regularization procedures influence the phenomenon, Moniri and Hassani [2026] show that, in the setting of linear ridge regression, the student can surpass the teacher by having better regularization. This is similar to the findings of Medvedev et al. [2025] and our work, but we use implicit regularization imposed by early-stopped gradient flow instead of explicit regularization. They also show that student regularization that is better aligned with the target function improves performance. They also go beyond linear models and frozen representations by considering a pretrained student in a nonlinear setting. The student learns easy, task-specific features from the teacher while retaining hard features acquired during pretraining that the teacher cannot capture, allowing it to outperform the teacher. 

Beyond linear. Going beyond linear settings, Oh et al. [2025] use CNNs for both the teacher and the student and adopt yet another characterization of model strength. They characterize it through the activation function: a linear activation for the weak teacher and ReLU for the strong student. Even in this setting, which differs from ours, they find that, in a data-abundant regime, early stopping the student training leads to better performance. 

Scaling laws. Finally, regarding the important problem of scaling laws, Ildiz et al. [2025] study ridge regression trained on the predictions of a surrogate model in the proportional high-dimensional regime. Using deterministic equivalents for the two-stage procedure, they characterize when the student improves on the surrogate and derive scaling laws in the sample size and model size. Wu et al. [2026] show that WTS can improve the scaling law of the test error in a random feature ridge regression 16 setting. Closely related to our work, they also derive a deterministic equivalent for the student test error.

## B.1 Background on the Dyson equation and Gaussian approximation 

In the random features setting we have two sources of randomness: the data distribution and the random features distribution. It turns out that when calculating the population error, the latter remains as a resolvent of a random covariance matrix. The concept of deterministic equivalents of random matrices can be useful in this case. The idea is to find a deterministic matrix around which this random resolvent concentrates. This deterministic matrix will have the same spectral information as its random counterpart when the dimension is very large and the reduced randomness makes the computations more tractable. One might consider the expectation matrix as a natural choice of deterministic equivalent but, as argued in Couillet and Liao [2022], this choice is not desirable since it involves an integration over a probability space which can be very challenging numerically. Instead, a more tractable deterministic equivalent can be derived through the associated matrix Dyson equation [Erd˝ os, 2019]. For a Gaussian random matrix A ∈ Rd×d the matrix Dyson equation for its resolvent R(z, A ) can be derived by applying the Stein’s lemma together with the tautological equation 

R(z; A)( A − zI ) = I (B.1) where I ∈ Rd×d is the identity matrix, and some concentration properties of the Gaussian distribution. This leads to a self-consistent equation for a scalar quantity, and by some technical stability results, one can show that the Dyson equation has a unique solution which is given in terms of this scalar quantity. In this case, both the data and the random features are sampled uniformly from the sphere. The Dyson equation framework, however, is derived using Gaussian integration by parts, or equivalently Stein’s lemma. We therefore invoke the Gaussian Universality Ansatz to connect the spherical ReLU model to a Gaussian model with the same relevant covariance structure. We explain heuristically why one should expect it, and then we discuss related literature where versions of this have been proven rigorously. 

Cumulant expansion heuristic. Stein’s lemma can be generalized to general random variables, in the form of a cumulant expansion . For a random variable X and a smooth enough function f

E[Xf (X)] = κ1(X)E[f (X)] + κ2(X)E[f ′(X)] + κ3(X)2 E[f ′′ (X)] + · · · , (B.4) where κi(X) is the i-th cumulant of X. Note that since all cumulants for k > 2 of the Gaussian distribution are zero, (B.4) gives Stein lemma when X is Gaussian. With that, what we are in fact supposing is that the higher cumulants of the random vector σ(U ⊤x) (for U a matrix of uniform spherical vectors and data vectors x) do not influence resolvent statistics; indeed, entries of this vector are affected by large collections of almost independent random variables which lead to central limit theorem effects. 

Related literature on the Gaussian Universality Ansatz. A central work in this area is Hu and Lu [2023], who prove that, under suitable assumptions in the proportional regime, nonlinear random feature models and Gaussian models with matching covariance have asymptotically equivalent training and generalization errors. Related results were obtained by Montanari and Saeed [2022], who establish covariance universality for the training error and the minimum test error among near empirical risk minimizers. The Gaussian equivalence principle was also studied by Goldt et al. [2022], who established rigorous conditions for single layer generative models and applied the Gaussian description to pre learned features and kernel methods. The work of Schr¨ oder et al. [2023] is particularly close to ours, since it combines Gaussian universality with deterministic equivalents for deep random feature models. They prove universality of the ridge regression test error when the learner and target share the same intermediate layers, and conjecture extensions to more general architectures and convex losses. 

Related literature on deterministic equivalent approximations. Deterministic equivalents can provide quantitatively accurate approximations to population errors. In particular, Defilippis et al. [2024] establish a dimension free nonasymptotic multiplicative approximation for random feature ridge regression under concentration and spectral assumptions. Their bounds give vanishing relative errors under suitable scaling of the sample size, number of features and regularization, even when the pop-ulation error itself vanishes. Moreover, their infinite data limit agrees with our teacher deterministic equivalent. Related nonasymptotic bounds for ridge regression and kernel ridge regression are given by Cheng and Montanari [2024] and Misiakiewicz and Saeed [2024], respectively. These results support expecting accurate deterministic approximations in our setting, although they do not directly con-trol our approximation errors. Our analysis considers a two stage learning procedure, where teacher generated labels introduce additional dependencies and cross terms. A closely related result is given by Wu et al. [2026], who derive a deterministic equivalent for the student excess error in a teacher student random feature ridge regression model, together with an additive nonasymptotic bound. Their result establishes relative accuracy when the approximation remainder is negligible compared with the student error.

## G.4 The degree-zero mode 

All figures were generated without the degree-zero mode of the kernel (4.1). The deterministic-equivalent curves use the spectrum over positive degrees, and the population-flow samples use the centered activation σ(r) = r+ − μd, whose kernel has no constant component.
