Abstract 

In this work, we study transformer attention through the lens of spectral geometry and operator theory. We view each attention head as a functional map between Hilbert spaces of functions on the token sequence and derive a Token Difference Operator , whose spectral structure controls how token-space information is routed to the output. We show that standard Euclidean spectra are structurally biased by sinks, conflating mass concentration with genuine routing capacity. By recasting token space in the intrinsic probability geometry induced by attention, the token difference spectrum disentangles sink effects from routing capacity and provides a spectral description of the dimensionality of the head output. This yields a unified framework for analyzing attention maps, explaining sinks, routing collapse, and output dimensionality within a single operator-theoretic framework. In practice, by grounding attention heuristics in spectral geometry, we develop a novel attention-based uncertainty estimator that complements probability-based scores, with the largest gains on long-context inputs. 

1 Introduction 

Attention maps are increasingly viewed as windows into the internal computation of transformer architectures [37]. Prior works have shown that attention carries useful signals about how trans-formers process information, studying it as probability distributions [37], graph operators [27, 7], transition matrices [14], or through row-wise statistics [38, 43]. While these perspectives have revealed important insights, they primarily focus on pairwise token interactions, or leveraging models that do not describe the single pass effect of the attention, leaving comparatively unexplored the global structural properties of attention operators as a whole, especially in relation to the hidden representation dimensionality and the final output of the transformer. In this work, we approach attention from this global operator perspective, framing each attention map A as a linear functional operator that transfers signals between geometric domains defined for the token sequence, and investigating its spectral properties. Inspired by functional maps [28] and shape difference operators [33, 20], we derive a functional operator that characterizes the single-step action V 7 → AV induced by an attention head. Its spectrum reveals the multi-scale, global behavior of attention and is, as we demonstrate, strongly correlated with a range of phenomena from attention sinks to model uncertainty. We introduce the Token Difference Operator (TDO), a framework that departs from standard Euclidean analysis by assigning distinct geometric structures to the input and output domains of the attention map A. We demonstrate that the conventional practice of adopting a uniform Euclidean metric is structurally flawed; specifically, it is biased by the inherent row-stochasticity of attention and the

Attention Interpretability. A broad line of work studies attention as a mechanism for information processing and propagation in transformers. The classical view interprets attention weights as soft similarity scores between queries and keys [37]. More recent approaches model attention as a discrete-time Markov chain [14], as a graph signal operator with an associated Laplacian [27, 7, 36], or through spectral properties such as QK eigenspectrum concentration and localization [2]. These methods reveal important structure, but they typically analyze attention as a probability matrix, graph, or repeated operator. Our work instead focuses on the single-step routing map V 7 → AV and studies its spectrum with respect to an intrinsic geometry induced by attention itself. This contribution is more relevant in the presence of attention sinks and representation collapse. Attention sinks have been studied through embedding spectra [8], information propagation [4], and geometric analyses [32], and related phenomena have also been observed in vision transformers [10]. In parallel, hidden-state collapse and compression effects have been linked to oversquashing and attention sinks [3, 11], as well as to spiking behavior in feed-forward layers [35, 12]. We build on this line of work by showing that standard Euclidean spectral diagnostics can confuse sink concentration with routing capacity, and by introducing an intrinsic spectrum that separates these effects. 

Uncertainty quantification in LLMs. Uncertainty estimation and hallucination detection for LLMs are often based on output probabilities, using quantities such as entropy, perplexity, likelihood, or sampling-based semantic consistency [44, 22, 13], and also spectral signals computed on the output logits [25, 40]. However, probability-based signals can be unreliable when task-relevant distinctions are compressed before reaching the logits [3, 29, 39]. This motivates uncertainty signals derived from internal model dynamics. For this reason, recent methods leverage hidden representations of LLMs to quantify uncertainty [23, 9]. In particular, some works focus on attention heads, looking at attention values [34], previous-token relations [38], Laplacian eigenvalues of attention graphs [7], and topological divergence measures on attention graphs [5]. While closely related to these attention-based methods, our approach replaces scalar or graph-based summaries with the intrinsic spectrum of the TDO. Our solution grounds uncertainty estimation in the same geometry that controls routing capacity, yielding a single-pass estimator that captures spectral collapse in token-level information flow.

4 Sinks, Routing, and Output Dimensionality 

We test two predictions of the theory: that the intrinsic TDO reduces sink bias, and that its centered spectrum tracks output dimensionality. We compare intrinsic spectral diagnostics ( PR( Dμ,π ) and its centered restriction) against (i) raw attention statistics , sink score, previous-token score [38], attention entropy [43]; and (ii) counting-geometry spectral diagnostics ,log-determinant score [34], Laplacian diagonal entropy [7], and PR( A⊤A). All metrics are evaluated against both sink score and the output spectral entropy H(AV ) [3] across the full head population. 

Representative heads Figure 2 compares four heads with clear routing properties that metrics should distinguish: a copying head, a sink head, a sink-biased head, and a shifting head. In the sink-biased head, substantial sink mass coexists with nontrivial routing, so counting-geometry and graph-based diagnostics confuse column-mass concentration with routing collapse, scoring it comparably to the sink head. The shifting head exposes the complementary failure: previous-token score [38] is tailored to this specific pattern and correctly flags it, but for the same reason misses the copying head, which routes information through a different position. In contrast, PR( Dμ,π ) assigns high routing scores to both copying, shifting heads, and sink bias, while separating them from sink collapse, matching the routed output dimensionality across all four cases. 5 Spectral Attention Uncertainty Quantification 

Probability-based uncertainty estimators, such as perplexity, sequence likelihood, and token entropy, are computed at the unembedding output, after the residual stream has been mapped to logits. When task-relevant distinctions are compressed during representation propagation, two inputs that differ in important ways induce nearly indistinguishable logits, and any output-probability estimator inherits this collapse [3, 29, 39], especially in long-range or non-local tasks. Figure 4 makes this concrete: on a controlled copy task, perplexity and token entropy become indistinguishable across two input families that still differ in a task-relevant position, while attention-based signals derived from hidden-layer 7routing geometry remain sensitive. The framework of Section 3 defines what the signal measures: the TDO spectrum quantifies the effective dimensionality of the centered, token-dependent component routed by an attention head. We hypothesize that spectral collapse during generation signals a loss of routing capacity that is not yet visible in output probabilities, and we test this hypothesis empirically. To this end, we define Spectral Attention Uncertainty Quantification (SAUQ), a single-pass estimator that turns intrinsic attention spectra into a confidence score for selective prediction.

5.2 Evaluation 

We evaluate SAUQ using Llama-3.1-8b[1] and Qwen2.5-7B [42] (Appendix B.3). Table 1 compares SAUQ performances against single-step probability-based methods (Perplexity, MaxSe-quenceProbability, MeanTokenEntropy, CCP), sample-based (10 samples) methods (NLI-based spectral measures, EigenScore, LUQ, LexicalSimilarity, SAR, Semantic Density, SemanticEntropy), and attention-based baselines (AttentionScore, RAUQ, LapEigvalRAUQ, LogDetRAUQ). The last two are variants of SAUQ in which the attention signal has been extracted based on LapEigval[7] and AttentionScore[34]. In Table 1, we report the mean PRR values for Llama-3.1-8b; We refer to the Appendix for additional results. The table shows a regime-dependent picture rather than uniform dominance. On QA datasets, SAUQ is competitive with strong unsupervised estimators, but it is not the best method on average; several sample-based baselines obtain stronger mean QA performance. In contrast, the gains are clearest on summarization, where SAUQ achieves the best performance on both SAMSum and XSum, and the strongest summarization mean among all methods considered. This is consistent with our motivation: when uncertainty depends on preserving distinctions across long contexts, the upstream routing spectrum carries information that probability-based estimators have already lost. We therefore view SAUQ as a complementary single-pass signal rather than a universal replacement. Because SAUQ, RAUQ, LapEigvalRAUQ, and LogDetRAUQ share the same recurrence and protocol, their comparison primarily reflects the effect of replacing the scalar attention signal used. The comparison among them isolates the effect of the routing metric inside the estimator. The ranking mirrors the metric-quality findings of Section 4: LapEigval and LogDet spectral signals carry less of the routing signal than the intrinsic TDO, and this gap translates into stronger single-pass uncertainty.

6 Conclusion 

We introduced a new spectral-geometric framework for attention. Euclidean spectral diagnostics can be biased by sink behavior, conflating column-mass concentration with genuine routing; the intrinsic probability geometry instead yields a token-difference spectrum that separates the trivial constant mode from the centered modes controlling routing capacity. Empirically, TDO operator better disentangle relations between sink score and head-output dimensionality, and supports uncertainty estimation. Building on it, SAUQ replaces scalar attention probes with a spectral measure of routing capacity and improves over closely related attention-based baselines on long-range tasks. 

Limitations. Our analysis is per-head, per-input, and single-step, characterizing the routing map before residual mixing, MLP updates, and cross-head output projection; extending it to the full dynamics of representation evolution is a natural next step. Practically, computing the full spectrum is more expensive than scalar attention statistics or the participation-ratio statistic used in our variant, requiring additional care to apply at scale. 

Broader Impact. More faithful diagnostics of attention routing may improve interpretability, uncertainty estimation, and reliability assessment in transformer models, especially for deployment of safe and reliable AI systems.
