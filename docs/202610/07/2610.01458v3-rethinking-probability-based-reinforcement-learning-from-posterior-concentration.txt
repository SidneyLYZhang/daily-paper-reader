## ABSTRACT 

Verifier-free reinforcement learning with probability-based rewards offers a promising way to train LLMs on general reasoning tasks where external verifiers are unavailable. Yet the reliability of these rewards, especially in long-horizon reasoning, remains underexplored. This work identifies a length-dependent fail-ure mode of probability rewards, which we call the Posterior Concentration Phe-nomenon (PCP). We show that the probability of a reference answer conditioned on a reasoning trace often collapses to a low-variance interval as the trace be-comes lengthy. This phenomenon results in nearly indistinguishable rewards, which, under GRPO-based settings, makes probability-based policy optimization unstable and inefficient. Motivated by this, we propose Reinforcement Learning with Concentration-aware Posterior Rewards (RLCPR) , a verifier-free RL frame-work to explicitly account for PCP for better optimization stability and token ef-ficiency. It has two components: uncertainty-aware data sampling , which re-duces concentration-prone rollouts before generation, and concentration-aware regularization , which penalizes unnecessarily long traces when posterior rewards collapse. Extensive experiments show that, alongside higher token efficiency, RL-CPR outperforms the state-of-the-art verifier-free RL baseline by up to 4.0% on six of seven benchmarks, including general-domain and mathematical reasoning challenges.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a prominent solution to advance the reasoning capabil-ities of large language models (LLMs) (Ouyang et al., 2022). By optimizing models on sampled rea-soning trajectories, RL lets a model learn from its own successes and fail-ures, and has delivered strong gains in mathematical reasoning (Hu et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026), code generation (Cui et al., 2025), and complex decision-making tasks. However, many existing RL ap-proaches for LLM reasoning rely on verifiable rewards, where correctness can be easily checked by pre-defined rules or by external verifier-purposed models (Lambert et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026). This re-quirement limits their applicability to broader reasoning settings where ground-truth verification is unavailable, ambiguous, or expensive. Recently, probability-based rewards have emerged as a 1arXiv:2610.01458v3 [cs.AI] 5 Oct 2026 Preprint promising verifier-free alternative (Yu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Instead of us-ing an external verifier, these methods directly use the LLM’s own probability estimates to construct rewards. A representative formulation is the posterior reward, which scores a reasoning trace ( z) by the model’s posterior probability of generating the ground-truth answer ( y∗) after conditioning on 

z, i.e., Rθ (z) = P (y∗ | x, z ; θ). Intuitively, this posterior probability-based reward encourages the model to learn reasoning traces associated with a higher probability of the reference answer. It pro-vides dense, self-contained supervision that extends beyond strictly verifiable domains and requires no external model during training. Despite their practical appeal, the mechanism behind posterior probability-based rewards is un-derexplored. It is unclear whether these rewards can reliably distinguish high- versus low-quality reasoning traces. This question is particularly important for group-relative RL algorithms such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), where training depends on the difference in the rewards across multiple rollouts for the same prompt. Recent evidence suggests a reason for concern. Welch et al. (2026) show that, once a model has produced a reasoning trace, the likelihood it assigns to an answer in-creasingly reflects consistency with that trace rather than the model’s belief in the answer itself. If posterior rewards inherit this behavior, a sufficiently long trace may lock in its answer regardless of whether the reasoning is sound. Building on this concern, this work identifies a length-dependent failure mode of posterior rewards, which we call the Posterior Concentration Phenomenon (PCP). Our exploration experiments show that, as reasoning traces grow longer, posterior rewards for traces ending in correct and incorrect answers become less distinguishable, while their cross-rollout variance also contracts. We further connect this empirical behavior to recent results on log-likelihood concentration in long LLM gen-erations (Bell et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026). Specifically, as sequence likelihoods concentrate, it causes the posterior probability of the reference answer to depend less on the specific reasoning tra-jectory. Under a mild condition motivated by empirical evidence on LLM branching factors (Yang et al., 2026), we show that different reasoning traces become increasingly likely to receive similar posterior rewards as the considered range of reasoning lengths expands. This reveals a fundamental limitation of posterior rewards for group-relative optimization: as their ability to reflect reasoning quality diminishes, the reward differences that GRPO relies on no longer indicate which rollouts are better, leaving the policy without an informative optimization direction. Worse still, since roll-outs tend to grow longer as training proceeds, the training dynamics are pushed deeper into the concentrated regime over time. PCP reframes how posterior-based RL should be designed. If posterior rewards lose discrimina-tive power as traces lengthen, a training framework should (i) avoid spending generation budget on prompts likely to produce concentrated rollout groups, and (ii) prevent the policy from drifting into the long-trace regime where posterior rewards no longer reflect reasoning quality. Existing meth-ods address neither directly. RLPR (Yu et al., 2025), for instance, discards rollout groups with low reward variance, but it does so without characterizing when or why such groups arise. Moreover, because filtering occurs only after rollouts are generated and scored, the generation cost of the dis-carded groups is wasted rather than avoided. Motivated by our analysis, we propose Reinforcement 

Learning with Concentration-aware Posterior Rewards ( RLCPR ), a verifier-free RL framework that explicitly accounts for PCP for better optimization stability and token efficiency. RLCPR contains two major components: (1) an uncertainty-aware data sampling method to efficiently sample infor-mative training data with a lower risk of PCP, (2) a regularization mechanism that explicitly accounts for PCP and penalizes unnecessarily lengthy reasoning traces. Rather than replacing the posterior re-wards, RLCPR improves their reliability by preserving useful posterior information while reducing the adverse effect of long-horizon concentration. Overall, RLCPR outperforms RLPR by up to 4.0 percentage points on six of seven benchmarks, including MMLU-Pro, TheoremQA, WebInstruct, MATH-500, Minerva, and AIME24. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify the Posterior Concentration Phenomenon (PCP), a length-dependent loss of answer-probability discriminability in long LLM reasoning traces, and connect it to the reward collapse in probability-based RL. • We provide the first theoretical formulation of posterior-reward collapse and support it empirically through diminishing reward separation and contracting reward variance. • We propose RLCPR, which explicitly accounts for PCP through uncertainty-aware data sampling and concentration-aware regularization. 2Preprint 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Reasoning and confidence reliability. Prior work examines whether language models’ confidence reflects answer correctness (Jiang et al., 2021; Kadavath et al., 2022). Explicit reasoning complicates this relationship. While Yoon et al. (2026) find that verbalized confidence can become better cali-brated as chain of thoughts (CoT) unfolds, Welch et al. (2026) show that answer-token probabilities in vision-language models can increasingly reflect consistency with the generated reasoning rather than correctness. This concern is reinforced by studies of reasoning faithfulness: CoT explanations can rationalize hidden biases (Turpin et al., 2023), and final predictions do not always depend on the stated reasoning (Lanham et al., 2023). Moreover, prior beliefs can induce confirmation bias in both rationale generation and answer prediction (Wan et al., 2025). These findings motivate exam-ining whether confidence reliably distinguishes reasoning quality. Our work studies this question in posterior-based RL, where rewards must remain relatively discriminative across reasoning traces, and identifies a length-dependent loss of this property through PCP. 

Verifier-free reinforcement learning. Probability-based RL uses the model’s own confidence in reference answers to provide supervision without external verifiers. JEPO (Tang et al., 2025) and NOVER (Liu et al., 2025) construct training signals from answer log-probabilities and token-level perplexities, respectively. Rita (Sun et al., 2026) leverages bounding box-based posterior probabil-ity in visual grounding tasks to regularize thinking-answer consistency of vision-language models. More directly related to our work, VeriFree (Zhou et al., 2025) rewards reasoning traces using pos-terior probabilities of reference answers, while RLPR (Yu et al., 2025) extends this approach to non-verifiable domains through length normalization and reward debiasing. These methods demonstrate the promise of probability-based supervision, but its reliability for long reasoning traces remains underexplored. We address this gap by analyzing posterior concentration and introducing RLCPR, which mitigates its effects through uncertainty-aware data sampling and concentration-aware regu-larization.

## 4 POSTERIOR CONCENTRATION PHENOMENON 

This section rethinks the concentration phenomenon of LLMs from the perspective of posterior probabilities. We are guided by two questions: Given two reasoning traces generated by the same model for the same prompt, does the posterior probability reflect their reasoning quality? If not, under what conditions does it become less informative for GRPO optimization? We explore with an empirical study in Sec. 4.1, before introducing our proposed Posterior Concentration Phenomenon (PCP) in Sec. 4.2. 4.1 WARM -UP : E XPLORATION EXPERIMENTS 

We begin by examining posterior rewards over progressively longer reasoning traces. Fig. 2 shows two consistent signatures on the benchmarks MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024) and GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023). First, posterior rewards for traces ending in correct versus incorrect answers become less distinguishable at longer lengths (Figs. 2a and 2b, blue vs. yellow). Second, posterior-reward dispersion contracts with length: the overall reward variance decreases (Fig. 2c), and the variance among rollouts within the same group also steadily declines (Fig. 2d). Together, these results show that long reasoning traces provide less discriminative rewards within the groups used for relative policy optimization. We refer to this length-dependent loss of discriminative power as the Posterior Concentration Phenomenon (PCP) . See Appx. E.1 for the details of this explo-ration experiment. 4Preprint

Above, LN serves as a probabilistic counterpart to the deterministic length index n in Lem. 4.1: uniformly sampling LN converts Lem. 4.1’s zero-density statement over reasoning lengths into an equivalent probability statement. Intuitively, Prop. 4.1 suggests that, as the considered range of reasoning length grows, posterior probabilities for independently sampled traces of similar lengths become more likely to take similar values when their residual differences are close. Importantly, this does not imply lower model confidence; rather, the posterior becomes less discriminative about the quality of the underlying reasoning trace. This observation complements recent findings that reasoning can distort probability-based confidence signals (Welch et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2026). It uncovers the limitation of answer posterior probabilities as supervision signals: internal confidence or consistency does not necessarily provide reliable discrimination of reasoning quality. From an RL perspective, this loss of discriminability weakens the learning signal provided by poste-rior rewards: long rollouts may receive similar rewards despite differences in their reasoning quality, potentially making the relative advantages less informative about reasoning quality while still incur-ring substantial generation cost. Since PCP is a probabilistic effect, this motivates a PCP-aware framework with two complementary directions: (1) preferentially sample training examples that are less prone to posterior concentration, and (2) discourage unnecessarily long reasoning once posterior difference collapses. We introduce these two components in Sec. 5. 

## 5 RLCPR 

To account for posterior concentration during policy optimization, we propose RLCPR, a novel verifier-free post-training framework with two core components: uncertainty-aware data sampling (UDS) and concentration-aware regularization (CR). 

Uncertainty-aware data sampling (UDS). Discarding less informative groups with low standard deviation, as done by Yu et al. (2025), is inefficient. We propose to use uncertainty-aware data sam-pling to reduce unnecessary rollouts that may potentially suffer from PCP. Our sampling strategy is motivated by an empirical association: questions whose rollout groups exhibit posterior concentra-tion tend to have higher direct-answer uncertainty during training. This suggests using uncertainty as a proxy for concentration risk and preferentially retaining lower-uncertainty examples. Appx. C presents the diagnostic experiment about this relation between uncertainty and concentration. Model and implementation. We evaluate RLCPR on Qwen3-4B-Base (Yang et al., 2025). Our implementation builds on the official RLPR (Yu et al., 2025) codebase in verl (Sheng et al., 2025) but we exclude the standard-deviation filtering. UDS and CR effect. Table 2 evaluates the individual and joint effects of UDS and CR. CR alone provides modest gains, whereas UDS alone slightly degrades performance on MATH-500 and Min-erva, possibly because it filters out some difficult but informative training instances. Importantly, UDS is not a static difficulty-based filter: it periodically re-estimates uncertainty under the current policy (every f = 20 steps) and dynamically reshapes the training distribution. Its effectiveness therefore depends on how the policy evolves. Without CR, the retained rollout groups may still suf-fer from PCP, causing their posterior rewards to concentrate and weakening the optimization signal that drives subsequent policy updates. CR complements UDS by augmenting concentrated posterior rewards with a length-aware penalty, thereby restoring a discriminative training signal within the affected groups and enabling the policy to learn more effectively from the UDS-selected samples. The improved policy can, in turn, yield more informative uncertainty estimates for subsequent UDS updates. This dynamic interaction may explain why combining UDS with CR achieves the best performance on all three benchmarks, improving over the baseline by up to 6.7% .

CR cold start and UDS scheduler Table 3 evaluates the CR cold start and the UDS annealing scheduler. Without the cold start, we apply CR with (λ, p δ , p α) = (0 .5, 0.8, 0.2) from the beginning of training. This reduces accuracy by 4.9, 8.6, and 7.9 percentage points on MMLU-Pro, Theo-remQA, and WebInstruct, respectively, suggesting that early regularization can hinder learning. Re-moving the UDS scheduler and fixing (τ1, τ 2) = (0 .3, 0.7) throughout training also lowers accuracy across all three benchmarks. Together, these results support our claim that concentration becomes increasingly significant as the training proceeds. Thus, delaying CR and gradually adjusting the UDS thresholds during early training provides a more effective way to mitigate concentration. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We investigate the reliability of posterior probability rewards for verifier-free RL and identify the Posterior Concentration Phenomenon (PCP), where rewards become less discriminative as reasoning traces grow longer. We characterize PCP empirically and provide a theoretical formulation connect-ing it to log-likelihood concentration. Guided by these findings, we propose RLCPR, which explic-itly accounts for concentration through uncertainty-aware data sampling and selective length regu-9Preprint larization. Experiments show that RLCPR outperforms RLPR on six of seven reasoning benchmarks while requiring fewer cumulative rollout tokens. These results highlight the value of concentration-aware training for improving both effectiveness and token efficiency in probability-based RL.

## E IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

E.1 PCP’ S EXPLORATION -S TUDY SETUP 

We analyze posterior concentration using Qwen3-4B-Base on MMLU-Pro and GPQA-Diamond. For each dataset, we randomly sample 128 questions and generate eight responses per question, yielding 1,024 (x, y, z ) instances. We use a maximum response length of 1,536 tokens. For Figs. 2a and 2b, we partition the traces according to answer correctness, 1{y = y∗}. ## G DISCUSSION ABOUT PCP’ S RESIDUAL CONDITION 

Our PCP analysis explicitly adopts convergence of the cross-trace residual difference as a suffi-cient condition. This assumption is motivated by Bell et al. (2024) and Yang et al. (2026), who together establish convergence of the length-normalized difference between negative log-likelihood and realized entropy, alongside empirical evidence of diminishing deviations and variability as gen-eration length increases. Connecting these findings to posterior rewards offers a useful perspective on verifier-free RL, although establishing when the required residual differences converge remains an open theoretical question beyond our scope. Our contribution is to characterize conditions un-der which posterior rewards become less discriminative across reasoning traces and examine their implications for policy optimization. Guided by this perspective, RLCPR accounts for concentra-tion through uncertainty-aware sampling and selective regularization to improve training efficiency and performance. Future work may strengthen this foundation by deriving sufficient conditions and finite-length bounds for residual-difference convergence. 17
