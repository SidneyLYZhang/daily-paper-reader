## ABSTRACT 

Recent successes of matrix- and curvature-based optimizers have renewed interest in how update geometry shapes learning. These methods normalize or precondi-tion updates, changing how different components progress during training. In deep matrix factorization, the geometry that slows gradient descent (GD) favors low-rank solutions by delaying the emergence of small singular modes. This raises the question of what remains of that spectral bias when normalization or curvature correction weakens or removes the slowdown. We study how optimizer geometry and depth jointly govern this behavior through a common framework for singular mode dynamics. Under explicit balance and alignment assumptions, we derive birth, saturation, and decay laws for Euclidean GD, coordinate-wise updates of SignGD and an instantaneous Adam approximation, spectral updates of Muon and cumulative Shampoo, and a block curvature model of K-FAC. The resulting pic-ture is not a simple ordering from stronger to weaker low-rank bias. To highlight, SignGD and ideal Muon eliminate the divergent birth barrier and drive unsup-ported modes to zero in finite time. Cumulative Shampoo initially retains GD’s depth-dependent barrier, then accumulated gradients produce a catch-up phase while making previously active modes increasingly persistent. Undamped K-FAC cancels the factorization-induced slowdown while preserving the ordering of the target singular values, whereas positive damping introduces a spectral threshold below which the slow GD phase laws reappear. These results give normalization, accumulated state, damping, and depth a direct interpretation as controls deter-mining when modes emerge, persist, and disappear during training. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

For much of modern deep learning, large scale optimization was dominated by SGD and Adam-like coordinate-wise methods, while matrix preconditioning and approximate curvature methods remained comparatively limited in practice. Their additional memory, numerical, and systems re-quirements made their advantages difficult to realize at scale (Martens & Grosse, 2015; Gupta et al., 2018). This landscape is beginning to change. Recent foundation models with multi-billion and trillion parameters (Team et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Khona et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2026) are effec-tively trained with spectral optimizers such as Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) and SOAP (Vyas et al., 2025) at substantially large model, data, and batch scales. This empirical shift raises a theoretical question. The implicit bias analysis of gradient descent (GD) shows that Euclidean geometry slows the progress of weak singular modes and thereby promotes low-rank solutions in deep matrix factor-ization (Arora et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021). Whereas spectral normalization and curvature correction are designed to reduce precisely this kind of anisotropy, in idealized limits they can weaken or even cancel the singular value dependent slowdown. Their recent success therefore raises the question of whether GD’s spectral bias induced by depth is lost, preserved in a weaker form, or replaced by a qualitatively different mechanism of mode selection. More broadly, we ask: 

When do modern optimizers merely accelerate a common learning trajectory, and when do they change which spectral components emerge, persist, or disappear?

Our theoretical analysis focuses on deep matrix factorization, a canonical special case in which the evolution of singular modes can be characterized explicitly. Specifically, in full observation re-gression, it exposes transient singular mode learning (Saxe et al., 2013; Soudry et al., 2018), while in underdetermined matrix sensing and completion, it permits direct study of which interpolating matrix is selected (Arora et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021; Rhee et al., 2026; Parviz et al., 2026). In contrast to the Euclidean geometry of GD, non-Euclidean optimizers transform the factorization-induced geometry in qualitatively different ways. SignGD and Adam apply coordinate-wise adaptive scaling (Bernstein et al., 2018; Kingma & Ba, 2014); Muon acts directly on the spectrum of matrix updates (Jordan et al., 2024) while Shampoo introduces history-dependent matrix preconditioning (Gupta et al., 2018); and K-FAC attempts to compensate for the parameterization through curvature using approximate natural gradients (Martens & Grosse, 2015). In our study, we categorize these geometries based on their updates into Euclidean (GD), coordinate-wise (SignGD and Adam), spec-tral (Muon and Shampoo) and curvature based (K-FAC). These methods can reach nearly identical training losses while producing weights with different spectra, representations, and performance. Recent studies have begun to identify optimizer-dependent feature and simplicity biases, including differences between Adam and GD in nonlinear models (Cattaneo et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Xie & Li, 2024) and between spectral and Euclidean updates in deep linear networks (Dragutinovi´ c& Ranganath, 2026). These findings further motivate the central question we posed, and answering this requires distinguishing how quickly modes emerge from how they settle or disappear, since an optimizer can affect these phases differently. Our approach separates the geometry induced by the factorization from the update rule and state maintained by the optimizer. The product dynamics first express how each layer updates combine into the dynamics of the effective matrix. Under explicit balance, target alignment, and state com-patibility assumptions, this description reduces to a common equation for each singular mode. The factorization determines how factor dynamics changes the mode amplitude, while the optimizer de-termines the update through its normalization, accumulated statistics, and damping. This gives a controlled setting in which these mechanisms can be compared without attributing differences to changing singular directions. Our analysis separates three aspects of spectral learning that are often conflated: the birth of initially small nonzero modes to reach a prescribed amplitude, saturation near their targets, and decay of unsupported modes whose target is zero. These phases need not respond to an optimizer in the same way. A method can permit rapid emergence while retaining a slow decay tail, and accumulated gradient history can change the rate at which an already learned component settles. Consequently, the emergence of small modes alone does not describe the spectral bias of an entire trajectory as studied in prior works. Our results make these distinctions explicit as shown in Fig. 1. Moreover, it also brings out how optimizer state parameters, including adaptive stabilization and curvature damping, interpolate between distinct rank selection regimes, and verifies that these 2Preprint differences persist in underdetermined matrix factorization and regression. Our contributions are: 

(1) A common framework for spectral learning. We express effective matrix dynamics through the layer updates and derive an exact mode reduction under explicit balance and alignment assump-tions. The reduction separates the contribution of the factorization from the optimizer update states. 

(2) Phase laws revealing how optimizer settings shape the bias of depth. We derive birth, satu-ration, and decay laws that distinguish normalization, accumulated history, and damping. GD and SignGD dynamics establish contrasting reference behaviors. Instantaneous Adam identifies how a positive stabilizer restores slow dynamics at small gradient magnitudes. Ideal Muon accelerates both emergence and removal of modes in finite time. For cumulative Shampoo, an exact accumulator identity quantifies the transition from initialization dominated to history dominated dynamics. For the specified K-FAC block model, an exact birth time decomposition separates an undamped contri-bution from a damping-weighted GD contribution. Together, these results identify when optimizer geometry preserves, modifies, or cancels the suppression of small modes induced by depth. 

(3) Numerical validation of phase laws and regime transitions. Controlled experiments in deep matrix factorization and regression verify the derived phase laws. These experiments demonstrate that settings commonly associated with numerical stability can change relative spectral progress, determining which components emerge, persist, and disappear during training. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Optimization geometry and solution bias. Optimization geometry influences which solution is selected among those that fit the training data (Wilson et al., 2017; Gunasekar et al., 2018). For Adam, implicit bias analyses establish margin characterizations in separable classification under specified optimizer and learning rate assumptions (Zhang et al., 2024; Gronich & Vardi, 2026). Nat-ural gradient provides another illustration of the role of geometry: under suitable rank conditions, its effective matrix dynamics can eliminate the dependence on depth that arises under Euclidean gradient flow (Kerekes et al., 2021; Bernacchia et al., 2018). These results motivate examining both the symmetries respected by an optimizer and the trajectory it induces. Our framework comple-ments this perspective by expressing changes in unobserved matrix directions through accumulated optimizer induced motion, while using mode dynamics to isolate the effects of different optimizers. 

Training dynamics in matrix factorization. Analyses of gradient flow and gradient descent con-nect spectral dynamics to low rank recovery and incremental rank growth under specified initializa-tion and measurement assumptions (Gunasekar et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2023). While depth strengthens the tendency of low rank bias, it is not always equivalent to minimizing a single Schatten norm, indicating that trajectory-level geometry is essential (Razin & Cohen, 2020). Recent Muon analyses characterize how polar normalization changes spectral learn-ing and simplicity bias in deep linear networks and linear attention (Dragutinovi´ c & Ranganath, 2026), and establish approximately uniform singular value growth under assumptions on LoRA fac-torization (Kang et al., 2026). For fully observed bilinear matrix factorization, Rhee et al. (2026) derive uniform factor singular value growth after alignment, and analyze oscillations caused by finite optimization steps. Complementary empirical comparisons show that Muon’s relative performance depends on the factorization problem and hyperparameter tuning (Parviz et al., 2026). Shampoo has been interpreted as an adapted version of spectral descent that retains information about stochastic-ity and the parameter trajectory (Eschenhagen et al., 2026). Building on these results, we compare mode birth, decay, and saturation within a common balanced aligned model. While most analyses study one optimizer in isolation, our analysis includes SignGD and simplified Adam, derives an exact accumulator identity for cumulative Shampoo, and quantifies the effect of additive damping in K-FAC. We give a broader discussion on related works in Sec. A.

This construction follows the exact mode analysis of deep linear networks (Saxe et al., 2013) and isolates the effects of different optimizer geometries. ## 7 CONCLUSION 

Our analysis shows that the spectral bias of depth depends on optimizer geometry and the stage of training. A surprising consequence is that rapid learning of weak components can coexist with rapid removal of unsupported ones, as illustrated by the Muon analysis. Conversely, accumulated history can accelerate the emergence of a mode while prolonging its persistence. These effects make a single ordering of optimizers by stronger or weaker low-rank bias inadequate. A further implication is that parameters associated with numerical stability also shape learning bias. In the analyzed models, Adam’s stabilizer, Shampoo’s accumulator initialization, and K-FAC’s damping determine where normalization, history, or curvature correction governs spectral progress. They can therefore change how strongly depth suppresses small modes, even within the same opti-mizer. Although the phase laws rely on balance and alignment assumptions, they identify concrete mechanisms to understand optimizer behavior. This perspective motivates evaluating optimizers through which components they accelerate, suppress, or preserve alongside the training loss, since comparable fit can conceal differences in their dynamics. ## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Deep linear dynamics and balancedness. Saxe et al. (2013) derive exact learning dynamics for deep linear networks under structured initial conditions, including a decoupled mode representation and equal strengths across layers. Their analysis supplies the direct precedent for our aligned phase model. Arora et al. (2018) show that linear overparameterization changes the effective optimization geometry and can act as a preconditioner. Du et al. (2018) study balancing properties of gradient flow in homogeneous models, while Arora et al. (2019) use balanced initialization to derive effective matrix and singular value dynamics for deep matrix factorization. These GD results motivate our setup, but conservation of the GD balance invariant generally does not hold for other optimizers. 

Low-rank bias and the limits of norm descriptions. Gunasekar et al. (2017) investigate implicit regularization in two-layer matrix factorization and conjecture a nuclear-norm characterization in a restricted commuting setting. Arora et al. (2019) extend the analysis to depth and derive singular-value-dependent mobility under balanced gradient flow, showing that depth strengthens the tendency toward low-rank solutions. Razin & Cohen (2020) construct matrix-completion problems in which norms diverge as the loss vanishes, demonstrating the limits of a universal norm-minimization ac-count. Li et al. (2021) instead develop a greedy low-rank learning interpretation under explicit assumptions. A complementary trajectory perspective is provided by Neural Tangent Kernel and lazy-training analyses, which characterize sufficiently wide networks through a linearization around initialization and an approximately fixed kernel governing function-space evolution (Jacot et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2019; Sabanayagam et al., 2022). In contrast, our analysis retains the nonlinear multiplicative parameterization and its evolving, optimizer-dependent spectral mobility, allowing us to compare the birth, saturation, and decay of singular modes before convergence. 

Optimization geometry and implicit bias. Wilson et al. (2017) demonstrate that adaptive opti-mization can select solutions with substantially different generalization from GD even when training fit is good. Gunasekar et al. (2018) characterize implicit bias for several optimization geometries in underdetermined linear regression and separable classification. These results establish the broader principle that optimization geometry affects solution selection. Deep factorization adds a parameter dependent product differential, so the geometry acting on the effective matrix changes with both the factors and optimizer state captured by our general trajectory identity in Proposition 1. 

Implicit bias of Adam and related adaptive methods. Cattaneo et al. (2023) use backward error analysis to characterize corrections to Adam and RMSProp dynamics, relating implicit regulariza-tion to hyperparameters and training stage. The classification literature builds on the maximum margin characterization of GD for separable data (Soudry et al., 2018). Wang et al. (2021) study limiting parameter directions in homogeneous classification under their adaptive flow assumptions. Zhang et al. (2024) establish a maximum ℓ∞ margin result for full batch Adam in separable lin-ear classification. Xie & Li (2024) connect AdamW, which includes decoupled weight decay, to 

ℓ∞-norm constrained optimization under full-batch assumptions. Vasudeva et al. (2026) analyze a difference in feature learning between Adam and GD in a Gaussian classification model with two layer ReLU networks. These results concern distinct losses, optimizer conventions, or notions of bias. Our squared loss analysis studies finite singular amplitudes and phase times without weight decay, so classification margin results do not directly determine its birth or saturation laws. 

Analysis of spectral descent, Shampoo, Muon and K-FAC. Fan et al. (2026) characterize spec-tral descent and Muon through margin maximization in multiclass linear classification. Chen et al. (2025) connect Muon with decoupled weight decay to spectral-norm-constrained optimization. Es-chenhagen et al. (2026) interpret Shampoo as spectral descent adapted to stochasticity and the pa-rameter trajectory. History dependence is therefore an established feature of this geometry. Our contribution is the exact first integral of the specified cumulative accumulator in the aligned fac-torization model and its consequences for phase times. That identity does not extend unchanged to exponential averages or different inverse powers. Kerekes et al. (2021) explicitly study natural gradient implicit bias in deep linear classification and matrix factorization. Under their Gaussian model and Jacobian rank condition, natural gradient flow for matrix completion matches direct op-timization of the effective matrix, leaving unobserved entries unchanged. This is a direct precedent for connecting geometry to motion outside the observed coordinates. Our block curvature analysis 15 Preprint quantifies the effect of additive damping on mode birth and decay, and distinguishes it from both full curvature and factored damping. These works provide important geometry and momentum prece-dents, while our phase laws concern squared loss, explicit stabilization or damping factors, and finite targets in factorized models. 

Analysis of Muon in matrix factorization. Kang et al. (2026) analyze spectral gradient flow in a simplified LoRA factorization setting, proving approximately equal spectral growth rates and con-vergence results under stated conditions. Dragutinovi´ c & Ranganath (2026) derive dynamics for simplified Muon in deep linear networks and linear attention and investigate how avoiding slow sequential learning changes simplicity bias. Rhee et al. (2026) analyze bilinear factorization with full observation, including uniform factor growth after alignment, a conserved quantity distinct from GD’s, and finite step oscillations near solutions. They also study alignment in simplified settings. These results already establish much of the ideal polar growth baseline. Our treatment places it alongside memory dependent adaptive flows and damped curvature flows, with separate statements for birth, saturation, and zero target decay. Parviz et al. (2026) report controlled optimizer com-parisons showing that Muon’s relative performance in factorization depends on the problem and hyperparameter tuning. This motivates separating exact polar flow predictions from practical imple-mentation effects and comparing recovery at a common training fit.

D.5 WHY THE ALIGNMENT ASSUMPTION IS APPROPRIATE 

Assumption 2 does not claim that arbitrary training trajectories become aligned with the target. It restricts the analysis to a canonical invariant subspace in which exact mode dynamics can be derived. This restriction is standard in analyses of deep linear networks and deep matrix factorization (Saxe et al., 2013; Gunasekar et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021). It has three useful properties. First, full-observation regression fixes a target basis through the singular directions of M . If the factors and optimizer states are initialized in compatible chain bases, the layer gradients remain in those bases. Second, fixing the same initial spectral configuration for every optimizer ensures that differences in mode evolution arise from the optimizer update and its state, rather than from different initial singular directions. Third, the aligned model retains the nonlinear multiplicative dependence of the effective modes on the factors. It therefore preserves the depth-dependent mobility responsible for the phase laws while removing singular-vector rotation from the comparison. 21 Preprint For spectral and curvature-based optimizers, the aligned subspace is preserved by their matrix-equivariant updates. For coordinatewise methods, the same construction is exact when the aligned basis is the parameter coordinate basis. The general matrix dynamics outside this invariant subspace are given in the previous appendix section. If the target contains repeated singular values, the singular basis is not unique within the corre-sponding degenerate subspace. We next consider a less structured setting in which the effective predictor is trained by underdeter-mined linear regression as described in Sec. F. This experiment tests whether the qualitative predic-tions persist once our simplifying assumptions Assumptions 1 to 3 are removed. Fig. 16 shows that the optimizers reach comparable training losses and converge to nearly identical final singular spectra. This agreement is important: the pronounced differences observed during training are not simply consequences of one method converging to a substantially different level of fit. Nevertheless, the stable and effective ranks evolve differently. Thus similar terminal predictors need not imply similar spectral learning dynamics. The singular value trajectories in Fig. 17 make this distinction particularly clear. GD exhibits pro-nounced separation between the emergence times of successive singular values, consistent with the factorization induced small mode barrier derived in the aligned setting. Adam substantially compresses these delays. Muon produces a markedly more synchronized growth of the singular spectrum, consistent with normalization reducing mode dependent mobility differences. Cumula-tive Shampoo also accelerates weaker spectral components after its preconditioner has accumulated sufficient history, while K-FAC produces comparatively smooth growth across the spectrum through curvature correction. These effects remain visible even though the singular vectors are not fixed and the data covariance couples the modes. The experiment therefore supports the interpretation of the theoretical phase laws as mechanisms governing transient spectral learning rather than merely properties of the special aligned model. Exact exponents depend on the aligned reduction, but the resulting qualitative distinctions persist in the more practical underdetermined regression problem. 47 Preprint
