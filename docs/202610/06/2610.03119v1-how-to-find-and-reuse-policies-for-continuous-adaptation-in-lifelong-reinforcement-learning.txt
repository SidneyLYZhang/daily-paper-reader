ABSTRACT 

In lifelong reinforcement learning, retaining previously learned policies is not suf-ficient for effective transfer to a new task. Useful knowledge may be distributed across several prior policies, and its relevance may change as the learner acquires experience. One hypothesis is that task similarity can be effectively used in a con-tinual learning setting to find and combine previously learned policies. To test it, Adaptive Mask Selection and Composition (AMSC) is designed to estimate sim-ilarity from online experience via non-parametric Wasserstein task embeddings from state-action-reward samples. The z-score-normalized sparsemax of the sim-ilarity scores are used to derive a variable-size support to periodically choose and weight policies to form a prior when learning a new task. On CT-graph and Min-iGrid, AMSC achieves higher mean performance and forward transfer than the evaluated modular composition baselines while exhibiting no forgetting. Results on Continual World suggest that identifying relevant prior knowledge and deter-mining its layer-specific composition may require additional layer-specific tuning. Ablations show that selecting relevant sources and determining how strongly to reuse them are central to these gains. Independently measured pairwise transfer is also positively associated with task-embedding similarity. These results indi-cate that task similarity can be an effective criterion to select and weight specific knowledge for reuse in lifelong reinforcement learning. 1

1 INTRODUCTION 

Lifelong learning requires both retention and reuse of prior knowledge. Although forgetting remains a central challenge, retention alone does not ensure positive transfer to later tasks. As experience accumulates, a model must identify relevant knowledge without allowing irrelevant or conflicting knowledge to cause interference. Prior work has extensively studied retention and increasingly evaluates forward transfer (Rusu et al., 2016; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Zenke et al., 2017; Mallya & Lazebnik, 2018; Rolnick et al., 2019; von Oswald et al., 2020), but commonly reuses shared or aggregated knowledge. Selective reuse remains comparatively underexplored. This distinction is especially consequential in reinforcement learning (RL). Transferred knowledge affects the actions taken and the state-action distribution from which the policy learns. An unsuit-able prior can bias exploration and induce negative transfer (Taylor & Stone, 2009; Ahn et al., 2025; Uchendu et al., 2023). Sparse rewards provide little early evidence that a policy is harmful (Gimel-farb et al., 2021). Moreover, useful structure may be distributed unequally across several priors.

Modular approaches can preserve completed policies, but reusing the full library may admit interfering priors. Learning how strongly to reuse each policy from the target objective can identify useful policies but may converge only after their opportunity to improve early learning has passed. Measured task similarity can instead act as a method of estimating the relevance of prior policies without optimization (Carroll & Seppi, 2005; Taylor & Stone, 2009; Li et al., 2019; Gimelfarb et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2025; Nath et al., 2026). In task-incremental lifelong RL, the availability of prior policies is determined by curriculum history, completed policies are frozen, and the number of useful sources is unknown. A suitable selector must therefore adapt to the current library. As experience is collected on a new task, both the selected knowledge and its contribution to learning may also change. 2 RELATED WORK 

Selective reuse in lifelong reinforcement learning. Forward transfer and forgetting are distinct objectives in lifelong RL. Preserving earlier tasks does not imply faster learning on later tasks (Wol-czyk et al., 2021; 2022). Prior work promotes selective reuse through sparse or compositional sub-networks (Sokar et al., 2022; Ostapenko et al., 2021), related and unrelated task sequences (Ke et al., 2020), selective replay (Isele & Cosgun, 2018; Huang et al., 2025), and adaptive structure alloca-tion (Lee et al., 2021; Dekhovich et al., 2023; Savadikar et al., 2026). These methods select data, capacity, or structures, often to determine what knowledge is worth retaining. 

Modular routing and composition. Modular lifelong RL isolates task-specific knowledge and transfers through routing or composition. Prior methods activate modules, select policy heads, infer contexts, or route adapters (Powers et al., 2022; Kessler et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024; Julian et al., 2025); others compose masks, modules, policies, or task vectors (Ben-Iwhiwhu et al., 2023; Kessler et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2025; Malagón et al., 2024). 2Preprint 

Task similarity and optimal transport. Task similarity predicts transfer and supports policy se-lection in RL (Carroll & Seppi, 2005; Taylor & Stone, 2009; Li et al., 2019; Gimelfarb et al., 2021), while transferability methods use representations, probe networks, likelihoods, or parameter sub-spaces (Zamir et al., 2018; Achille et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025). Optimal transport compares task distributions through OTDD and Wasserstein embeddings (Courty et al., 2018; Alvarez-Melis & Fusi, 2020; Liu et al., 2025), and supports memory, selection, distillation, context detection, and expert diversity (Wang et al., 2022; 2024; Xu et al., 2023; Dick et al., 2024; Hihn & Braun, 2022). 

3 PRELIMINARIES 

3.1 PROBLEM SETTING 

Consider a task-incremental lifelong RL setting with a sequence of K tasks {T k}Kk=1 . Each task is an MDP Tk = ( S, A, p k, r k, γ rl ), where S and A are the state and action spaces, pk is the transition kernel, rk is the reward function, and γrl is the discount factor. Tasks arrive sequentially with known boundaries, and only the current task environment is available for interaction. The learner trains on each task for a fixed budget before proceeding to the next task. A lifelong learning agent aims to maintain performance on previously learned tasks, or improve it through backward transfer, while continuing to learn new tasks through forward transfer. Forward transfer measures whether knowledge acquired from earlier tasks accelerates learning of a new task (Parisi et al., 2019; Khetarpal et al., 2022; Wolczyk et al., 2021). 3.2 MODULAR POLICY MASKS

CKA-RL is a related modu-lar composition method that combines and merges dense task-specific residuals rather than binary masks (Hu et al., 2025). 5 RESULTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks. We evaluate AMSC on CT28, a sparse-reward decision-tree curriculum with hierar-chical tasks (Soltoggio et al., 2023); MG16, sparse-reward MiniGrid navigation tasks with shuffled observations (Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2018); and CW10, Continual World continuous robotic con-trol tasks (Wolczyk et al., 2021). In CT28 and MG16, tasks within each family share a common observation space and increase in complexity. Families differ in their representations while sharing input dimensions and actions. These curricula test reuse when prior knowledge varies in relevance in relation to the next task. Details and pairwise-transfer matrices are reported in Appendix C. 

Baselines. We compare against replay methods (SER (Isele & Cosgun, 2018) and SDW (Huang et al., 2025)), modular methods (Mask-LC/BLC (Ben-Iwhiwhu et al., 2023), CKA-RL (Hu et al., 2025), CompoNet (Malagón et al., 2024), OWL (Kessler et al., 2022), SANE (Powers et al., 2022), MOSAIC (Nath et al., 2026), and ProgNet (Rusu et al., 2016)), fine-tuning, and learning from scratch. We also evaluate retrieval guided by independently measured pairwise transfer. Tables 1 and 2 report comparisons to baselines on CT28, MG16 and CW10. Appendix E provides details of the hyperparameters for AMSC and baselines. 

Protocol and Metrics. All empirical results are evaluated with seven shared seeds. CT28 and MG16 use PPO with 51 , 200 and 512 , 000 steps per task, respectively. CW10 uses SAC with 1M 

steps per task. We report mean task area under the evaluation curve (AUC), forward transfer (FWT), and forgetting (FGT) from normalized performance between [0 , 1] , following (Wolczyk et al., 2021). We report these metrics together because zero forgetting can reflect strong retention but not neces-sarily strong policies. Appendix E specifies training and evaluation hyperparameters, Appendix F.3 reports hyperparameter sensitivity, and Appendix G reports the details of how the metrics are com-puted. 5.2 COMPARISON TO BASELINES 

Selective reuse improves learning. AMSC obtains strong AUC and FWT with zero measured forgetting on CT28 and MG16 (Table 1), occupying a favorable region of the transfer-retention trade-off (Figure 1) with respect to baselines. AMSC achieves AUC/FWT of 0.94 /0.94 and 0.73 /0.54 on CT28 and MG16, respectively. From-scratch learning also avoids forgetting but obtains only 0.22 

and 0.23 AUC. Surprise-prioritized SER slightly exceeds AMSC on CT28 ( 0.96 /0.95 ) but incurs 

0.54 FGT, while reservoir SER obtains 0.63 /0.20 on MG16 with 0.07 FGT. 

Comparison with modular reuse. AMSC achieves the highest mean AUC and FWT among mod-ular methods on CT28 and MG16 (Table 1). These methods differ in how they reuse prior knowl-edge: Mask-LC/BLC learn weights over all prior masks, CompoNet learns to combine policy-unit outputs, and MOSAIC selects masks through absolute similarity and performance gates before opti-mizing composition. AMSC instead periodically updates both its selected sources and their weights using library-relative similarity. On CT28, AMSC achieves AUC/FWT of 0.94 /0.94 , compared with 0.81 /0.79 for MOSAIC and 0.69 /0.64 for CompoNet. On MG16, both baselines have nega-tive FWT, whereas AMSC achieves 0.54 . AMSC’s gains over Mask-LC, CompoNet, and MOSAIC are significant on both metrics and curricula ( p ≤ 0.023 ). Does fixed similarity weighting extend to continuous control? On CW10, AMSC obtains 0.48 

AUC with zero measured forgetting, but its FWT is −1.21 ; Mask-BLC obtains 0.53 AUC and 0.12 

FWT (Table 2). Their composition weights overlap on some tasks, yet AMSC often allocates more mass to prior masks later in the curriculum. At task 9, mean actor prior mass is 0.78 for AMSC versus 0.46 for Mask-BLC (Appendix F.2). AMSC applies the same similarity-derived proportions across masked layers, whereas Mask-BLC learns layer-specific weights. This difference suggests that identifying relevant prior policies and determining how to compose their knowledge are distinct challenges, even when learned composition addresses both through a single set of weights. 5.4 TASK REPRESENTATION SENSITIVITY

6 DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK 

The results suggest that task similarity can guide effective policy reuse without directly optimizing retrieval or composition weights when relevant prior knowledge is available. On CT28 and MG16, AMSC achieves competitive AUC and FWT relative to replay methods while avoiding forgetting through per-task masks. The random-selection ablation indicates that sparsity alone is insufficient. Periodic retrieval further improves reuse as more experience is collected and task representations are updated. The differing performance of transition embeddings and Fréchet moments also suggests that repre-sentation choice depends on the distributions. Future work could evaluate combinations of priors beyond pairwise transfer to determine how reliably embedding similarity predicts joint transfer. The CW10 results highlight a distinction between identifying relevant knowledge and determining how to reuse it. Similarity-derived weights shared across layers may be too restrictive for effective com-position. Future work could test whether task embeddings can guide layer-specific reuse or whether similarity-based selection could be combined with learned composition, including an adaptive bal-ance between prior and current knowledge. Another direction is determining when an agent should abstain from reuse when no relevant prior knowledge exists. This work examines how sparsity and selectivity can enable effective forward transfer as the policy library grows, while managing that growth remains a complementary challenge. Since available knowledge influences what can be effectively learned, consolidation and compression should be evaluated for their effects on future transfer as well as retained performance. Finally, extending AMSC beyond known task boundaries requires detecting distribution changes and deciding when to retrieve, adapt, or create a new mask. 

7 CONCLUSION 

This work introduced Adaptive Mask Selection and Composition (AMSC) to address how lifelong agents can selectively reuse prior knowledge when learning new tasks. AMSC uses task similarity to periodically select and compose a sparse set of policy masks, without directly optimizing selec-tion or composition. On CT28 and MG16, it outperforms modular baselines in mean AUC and forward transfer and remains competitive with replay methods while retaining prior policies without forgetting. These findings show that task similarity can provide an effective alternative to learning compositions, with ablations supporting the importance of relevant selection and updating reuse dur-ing learning. The advantage is not universal, however, as the CW10 results suggest that identifying relevant prior knowledge and adapting its composition across layers may require different mecha-nisms. The effectiveness of similarity-guided reuse depends on how prior knowledge is composed per-layer. Together, the results position task similarity as a practical approach for selective reuse. Motivation and relation to binary supermasks. AMSC represents task i in layer ℓ by a real-valued score tensor Sℓi over a frozen weight tensor W ℓ. The forward pass uses a binary gate 

M ℓi = I[Sℓi ≥ 0] , hℓ+1 = σ (W ℓ ⊙ M ℓi )hℓ , (10) and propagates gradients to Sℓi with a straight-through estimator (STE). This follows Wortsman et al. (2020); Ben-Iwhiwhu et al. (2023), which optimize a subnetwork over a frozen backbone. Related signed-supermask work allows connections to be kept, hidden, or inverted (Koster et al., 2022). AMSC instead keeps the deployed gate in Equation 10 binary. It retains continuous scores for the linear combination before the gate is applied in the forward pass on the resulting mask. Score magnitude can therefore affect composition even though only the score sign determines an individual task’s subnetwork. Wortsman et al. (2020) also superimposes learned binary masks, but optimizes their mixture to infer a task at evaluation time. AMSC composes completed policies during target-task learning, then consolidates the composition as the next reusable policy. The following experiments test whether continuous score information is required for this transfer mechanism and whether completed masks can instead be stored in quantized form.

Joint normalization is therefore required for sparsemax weights to control actual mask contributions.

C BENCHMARKS 

For CT28 and MG16, we use family to denote tasks that share an observation encoding and a nested task construction across depths. All tasks within each benchmark have the same formal observation dimensions and action interface. Cross-family tasks differ in their observation dataset or symbolic remapping, rather than in tensor shape, and can still share behavioral structure that supports policy transfer. 

CT-Graph CT28. CT-Graph is a configurable decision-tree environment for continual RL (Soltoggio et al., 2023). At each decision state, the agent selects a branch and receives a sparse reward of 1 only after reaching the target terminal node. Each state is represented by an image gen-erated from a family-specific image dataset. Datasets do not overlap across families, whereas tasks within one family share observations for their common graph prefix. CT28 is based on the evaluation setup used in (Nath et al., 2026), and contains four families with depths 2 through 8, giving 28 tasks. Within each family, the solution at depth d forms a prefix of the solution at depth d + 1 . A depth-8 target is encountered after 24–27 previous tasks. Six of the available policies are lower-depth members of the target family, and the remaining policies belong to other families. CT28 therefore tests retrieval from a library in which relevant predecessors form a minority that decreases as the curriculum progresses. 

MiniGrid MG16. MiniGrid provides partially observable grid-world navigation tasks with sym-bolic object, color, and state observations (Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2018). MG16 extends Mul-tiRoom into four room-chain families. Each family contains tasks R2 through R5. Under a fixed layout seed, R d + 1 preserves the complete R d layout and adds one room and connecting transi-tion. The agent receives sparse reward for reaching the goal in the final room. The action space and physical task remain fixed across families. MG16 assigns each family a distinct symbolic remapping of wall, door, and floor cells. The remap-ping changes the observed object type and colour but preserves collision, traversal, and door-toggle affordances. Each remapping remains fixed across depth, so lower-depth policies share the target family’s observation code. Figure 5 shows the four profiles. Tasks are ordered by room depth, giving four R2 tasks, followed by four R3, R4, and R5 tasks. No family identifier is included in the policy observation. handle-press-side , push , shelf-place , window-close , and peg-unplug-side .Each task is trained for 1M environment steps with SAC, giving 10M steps in total. CW10 pro-21 Preprint vides shaped rewards and four-dimensional continuous actions. It therefore tests AMSC beyond the sparse-reward, discrete-action conditions of CT28 and MG16. Our experiments use the current MetaWorld-v2 goal-observable task definitions (Yu et al., 2020b). Each observation is a 39-dimensional state vector, and no one-hot task label is appended. Initial object and goal configurations remain randomized across episodes. Evaluation uses binary task success rather than shaped return. An episode is successful if the envi-ronment reports success at any step before termination. Every 50 ,000 training steps, the determinis-tic policy is evaluated for ten episodes on every task encountered so far. Each evaluation uses a fresh environment and a task-specific seed, and does not alter the training environment or replay buffer. The resulting success-rate matrix supplies the CW10 AUC, FWT, and FGT measurements.

D BASELINES 

Comparison protocol. All methods use seven seeds, identical task orders, and the same interac-tion budget. CT28 and MG16 use the common PPO architecture and optimizer settings reported in Section 5. Task labels are used at task switches and evaluation but are not included in policy obser-vations or policy selection. CW10 uses the SAC schedule and evaluation protocol in Appendix C. Architectures are matched where the algorithms permit direct substitution. CompoNet and ProgNet retain their released two-layer, 256-unit ReLU models and a matching no-reuse run is used to calcu-late their FWT. Other CW10 methods use the four-layer, 256-unit CW architecture with first-layer LayerNorm. 

Learning references and regularization. Fine-tuning updates one policy throughout the curricu-lum without a retention mechanism. No-reuse trains an independent policy on each task and provides the zero-transfer learning curves used to calculate FWT. 

Replay. SER (Isele & Cosgun, 2018) maintains short-term FIFO memory and bounded episodic memory. We report surprise-prioritized and reservoir-sampled SER variants. SDW (Huang et al., 2025) estimates task similarity and uses it to control replay and policy/value consistency weights. These methods select or weight stored experience rather than stored policy modules. 

Parameter isolation and routing. ProgNet (Rusu et al., 2016) adds a frozen policy column for each task and learns lateral connections from earlier columns. OWL (Kessler et al., 2022) combines task-specific heads with oEWC on shared features and selects a head using an exponential-weights bandit. SANE (Powers et al., 2022) maintains an ensemble of actor–critic modules and activates a module using optimistic value uncertainty. Our task-incremental SANE configuration clones the selected module at each task boundary and freezes earlier modules. 

Policy composition. Mask-LC and Mask-BLC (Ben-Iwhiwhu et al., 2023) learn softmax coeffi-cients over the current mask and every earlier mask. Mask-LC initializes the active coefficients uni-formly. Mask-BLC initially allocates half of the mass to the current mask and divides the remainder among the earlier masks. CKA-RL (Hu et al., 2025) learns task-specific parameter residuals around a shared policy and fuses residuals into a bounded knowledge pool. CompoNet (Malagón et al., 2024) adds one policy unit per task and uses learned attention to combine the outputs of frozen earlier units with the current unit. These baselines learn routing or composition without first con-structing a library-relative sparse support. 

Similarity-guided composition. MOSAIC (Nath et al., 2026) was introduced for knowledge ex-change among concurrently learning agents. We adapt MOSAIC as a baseline, limiting candidates to frozen masks from earlier tasks but retaining MOSAIC’s absolute cosine threshold, performance gate, learned composition, and acquisition protocol. A successful acquisition consolidates the active composition and reinitializes its coefficients from source and target performance.
