# On the Convergence of Success Conditioning for Policy Optimization 

## Matthew Brun ∗ Xu Andy Sun †

Abstract 

Success conditioning is a strategy for improving decision-making policies in stochastic environments; it updates a policy by increasing the probability of taking actions that yield successful outcomes. Success conditioning is common to many reinforcement learning applications, yet its limiting behavior and con-vergence rates are not well understood. In this work, we demonstrate that success conditioning converges to an optimal policy on a broad class of Markov decision processes (MDPs). We also derive convergence rates in some common settings. For discounted MDPs, we prove convergence within O(1 /ε p) iterations to an ε-optimal policy, where the exponent p depends on problem data. For single-period MDPs, such a policy is obtained within O(log(1 /ε )) iterations. 

# 1 Introduction 

Success conditioning (SC) is an update rule for policies in stochastic sequential decision-making processes. SC improves a randomized policy by increasing the probability of taking actions that yield high rewards and decreasing the probability of taking those which are not fruitful. When outcomes of the process are labeled as successes and failures, the policy update mimics the distribution of actions which yield successful outcomes. SC and related strategies appear across modern artificial intelligence (AI) contexts, especially in machine learning and reinforcement learning [1, 2]. Rejection sampling fine-tuning refines generative models by sampling outputs, discarding those with low-quality, and continuing to train on the best responses [3]. Post-training pipelines for large language models apply this strategy [4], and similar concepts are embedded directly in the training process via reinforcement learning [5]. Models for robot control have been trained on successful trajectories from other robots [6] and can autonomously fine-tune on successful outcomes from practice sessions [7]. In reinforcement learning, decision transformers learn to predict actions as a function of downstream rewards, then identify high-quality actions by conditioning on large returns [8]. The SC update was formalized by Russo [9] for a Markov decision process (MDP) where rewards are received at a terminal state. Equivalent updates for dense rewards appear in work on outcome-conditioned behavioral cloning [10] and expectation-maximization algorithms for MDPs [11, 12]. SC is closely related to 

reward conditioning , which updates policies by conditioning the action distribution on a reward threshold [13, 14]. In previous work, SC has primarily been treated as a heuristic method for policy improvement. Convergence of the SC update has not been rigorously derived and was recently posed as an open question by Russo [9].

In contrast, we prove explicit iteration complexities for SC, parametrized by the initial policy. An extensive comparison with ˇStrupl et al. [12] is provided in Appendix A.

## 2.2 Motivation by Conditional Probability under Binary Rewards 

The SC update is motivated by a setting with binary rewards that are received at the termination of an undiscounted MDP [9]. This setting is a restriction of the one presented above, in which: a) there exists a set of terminal states T ⊂ S ′ that transition directly into the absorbing state ( P (s | τ, a ) = 1 for all a ∈ A 

and τ ∈ T ); b) rewards are only received in terminal states ( R(s, a ) = 0 for s̸ ∈ T ), are independent of the action ( R(τ ) = R(τ, a ) for all τ ∈ T and a ∈ A ), and are binary ( R(τ ) ∈ { 0, 1} for τ ∈ T ); and c) the MDP is undiscounted ( γ = 1). # 4 Convergence Rate for Single-Period MDPs 

In this section, we consider an MDP that consists of two states ( S = {s◦, s }) and reaches the absorbing state s immediately after the first action ( P (s | s◦, a ) = 1 for all a ∈ A ). Under this structure, Qπ (s◦, a ) = 

R(s◦, a ). As the action taken in the first period entirely determines the infinite-horizon reward, we refer to this problem as a single-period MDP. Single-period MDPs are multi-armed bandit problems in which the rewards associated with each arm are known exactly, and are equivalent to maximally-discounted MDPs, i.e., those with γ = 0. Although single-period MDPs can be solved in closed-form by selecting an action which maximizes R(s◦, a ), they provide a simple and instructive setting for analysis of SC convergence rates. With a similar perspective, Russo [9] proposes an example of a single-period MDP that demonstrates how the SC update may make slow progress towards an optimal policy. For single-period MDPs, we derive a rate of convergence of the value function Vk(s◦) to the optimal restricted value function V ∗(s◦) = max a∈A 0(s◦) R(s◦, a ). # 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

Theorem 1 establishes that SC converges to a restricted optimal solution for well-behaved MDPs satisfying Assumption 1. Theorem 2 shows that SC converges to an ε-optimal policy within O(log(1 /ε )) iterations for single-period MDPs, and Theorem 3 shows that SC converges within O(1 /ε p) iterations for discounted MDPs, with an exponent p ≥ 1 that depends strongly on problem data and may be arbitrarily large. These results address several open questions on the convergence behavior of SC. Our convergence rates give upper bounds on the worst-case performance of SC; SC may converge faster on typical instances, and further analysis may prove convergence at a faster rate. Relative to comparable policy optimization algorithms, the SC convergence rates presented in this work are slow. For single-period MDPs, selecting the action with the largest reward recovers an exact optimal policy in a single iteration. For discounted MDP, value iteration and policy iteration both converge linearly, yielding an ε-optimal policy in O(log(1 /ε )) iterations [16]. Policy iteration may even converge quadratically, requiring only O(log(log(1 /ε ))) iterations [16, Thm. 6.4.8]. The slow convergence of SC can be viewed as a feature. Russo [9] describes SC as a “conservative” operator that does not deviate significantly from the incumbent policy π0. Changes in the policy under the SC update directly correspond to improvements in its performance [9, Prop. 4.4]. Although this conservatism inhibits efficient convergence, it permits stability in learning by avoiding drastic changes in the distribution of actions. SC also exhibits stability by converging to an optimal restricted solution, and cannot produce policies that take actions which were not taken by the initial policy π0.Another strength of SC is that it generates stochastic policies, in which actions that were supported under the initial policy remain supported at every iteration. In contrast, value and policy iteration generate policies that deterministically select a single action. Stochastic policies are desirable for many applications, especially in the context of generative AI [17, 18]. Additionally, stochastic policies permit on-policy learning, where the Q-functions Qk(s, a ) are approximated by sampling trajectories under the incumbent policy πk. As SC maintains non-zero probabilities on the set of supported actions, a sufficiently large number of trajectory samples will visit all supported state-action pairs, permitting an estimate of the Q-functions. Because deterministic policies always select a single action, value and policy iteration are not compatible with Q-function estimation by on-policy sampling. Similar properties (conservatism and stochastic policy iterates) are shared by trust-region policy opti-mization [TRPO; 19] and proximal policy optimization [PPO; 20]. However, these methods require parameter tuning and generally do not have well-understood convergence rates, although some variants converge in 12 O(1 /ε 2) iterations [21]. For comparison to TRPO and PPO, interesting open directions include designing on-policy variants of SC that estimate the Q-functions from trajectory samples and investigating the sample complexity of these variants.
