## Abstract 

While the maximal coding rate reduction (MCR 2) objective has become a useful principle for learning compact and discriminative representations, a theoretical understanding of its optimization geometry remains limited, especially on why first-order methods often exhibit fast convergence near its local maximizers. In this work, we show that the MCR 2 objective satisfies an error-bound property in a neighborhood of its local maximizers. This property guarantees that the distance to the local maximizer set can be bounded above by the gradient norm at any point in the neighborhood, directly implying regularity conditions such as the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality and quadratic growth. Leveraging this property, we prove that first-order methods converge linearly to a local maximizer of the MCR 2

objective under mild conditions. Numerical experiments validate the predicted local convergence behavior and provide empirical evidence that optimizing the MCR 2 objective improves representation geometry while maintaining competitive downstream accuracy. 

## 1 Introduction 

In deep representation learning, the maximal coding rate reduction (MCR 2) objective [Ma et al., 2007, Yu et al., 2020], grounded in the lossy coding rate, has attracted increasing attention in recent years. Thanks to the remarkable performance in learning compact and structured representations that facilitate downstream tasks, MCR 2-based approaches have been widely applied to multi-class classification [Wen et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2020], image segmentation [Chan et al., 2022, Meng et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2024b], and image and language generation [Yu et al., 2024a, Wang et al., 2025]. In particular, neural network architectures obtained by unrolling algorithmic updates for optimizing the MCR 2 objective, such as ReduNet [Chan et al., 2022], CRATE [Yu et al., 2024a], and their variants [Yang et al., 2024, Pai et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2025a], have been demonstrated to work effectively in practice. As a guiding principle for learning linear discriminative representations, the MCR 2 objective has achieved remarkable empirical success. Nevertheless, due to the highly complicated and non-convex nature of the objective (see Problem (3)), the theoretical understanding of its optimization

A seminal work [Chan et al., 2022] proved that the global solutions of the MCR 2 problem are within-class compressible, between-class discriminative, and maximally diverse. Recently, Wang et al. [2024] explicitly characterized the set of local maximizers of the regularized MCR 2 problem. Moreover, they showed that this problem has a benign loss landscape in the sense that every critical point is either a local maximizer or a strict saddle point (see Fact 1). More interestingly, they observed that gradient ascent applied to this problem can achieve linear convergence, but a formal theoretical justification remains elusive. In addition, MCR 2-based neural network architectures have been shown to be effective at learning feature representations [Chan et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2024a]. These observations naturally raise the question of whether the MCR 2 objective possesses favorable optimization properties that ensure efficient convergence when optimizing it, while effectively learning meaningful feature representations when unrolled as a neural network. 

1.1 Main Contributions 

In this work, we study the local optimization geometry of the MCR 2 objective around its local maximizers. This local perspective is important: since prior work shows that first-order methods with random initialization almost surely avoid strict saddles [Lee et al., 2016, 2019], the key question is not only where the iterates may converge, but also how regular the landscape is once the trajectory enters a basin of a local maximizer. Our work addresses this question by establishing local regularity of the MCR 2 landscape and using it to explain the fast local convergence of first-order methods near local maximizers. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• Despite the complexity and inherent non-convexity of the MCR 2 problem, we prove that it satisfies a local error-bound property [Luo and Tseng, 1993, Pang, 1997, Zhou and So, 2017] in a neigh-borhood of its local maximizer set; see Theorem 1. Specifically, we show that for any point Z in this neighborhood, the distance from Z to the local maximizer set is controlled by the gradient norm. This error-bound property captures favorable local geometric regularity of the objective and further implies standard regularity conditions, including the Polyak–Lojasiewicz (PŁ) inequality [Karimi et al., 2016, Polyak, 1963] and quadratic growth [Bonnans and Ioffe, 1995, Drusvyatskiy and Lewis, 2018]. 

• Leveraging the error-bound property and the benign loss landscape of the MCR 2 problem (see Fact 1), we show that first-order methods for optimizing the MCR 2 objective converge to a local maximizer at a local linear rate (see Theorem 2). This result explains the fast local convergence of gradient-based methods observed in practice and shows that, once the iterates enter a neighborhood of the local maximizer set, the MCR 2 objective has sufficient regularity to ensure geometric decay of the optimization error. It further provides theoretical support for MCR 2-based representation learning methods and unrolled architectures [Chan et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2025b, Pai et al., 2024], where first-order updates are used as building blocks for learning discriminative feature representations. To support our theory, we conduct numerical experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets. The results corroborate the predicted local convergence behavior and further illustrate the connection between optimizing the MCR 2 objective and learning discriminative feature representations. 

1.2 Related Works Deep representation learning using MCR 2. Current approaches using MCR 2 for learning repre-sentations can be broadly categorized into two types. The first uses MCR 2 as a training objective to learn discriminative and compact representations for different tasks. For example, an early work [Ma et al., 2007] demonstrated the effectiveness of the MCR 2 objective in classical clustering problems. Recently, Yu et al. [2020] optimized the MCR 2 objective to train a neural network for multi-class classification tasks. The work of Pai et al. [2023] leverages MCR 2 to learn discriminative representa-tions for data drawn from multiple subspaces by formulating the problem as a sequential game. The work of Meng et al. [2025] applies MCR 2 in temporal rate reduction clustering to segment human motion sequences. The second approach is to unroll neural networks based on algorithmic updates for optimizing the MCR 2 objective or its variants. For example, Yu et al. [2020] proposed a neural network named ReduNet by unrolling gradient ascent for optimizing the MCR 2 objective, enabling interpretable and 2efficient representation learning. Recently, Yu et al. [2024a] designed a transformer-like network architecture named CRATE by unrolling alternating minimization for optimizing the sparse rate reduction objective. Using different objectives or different optimization algorithms, Wang et al. [2025] proposed an attention-only transformer-like architecture, while Wu et al. [2025a] developed a linear-time variant of a transformer-like architecture. Please refer to [Pai et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2025b, Yang et al., 2024] for additional neural network architecture designs based on the MCR 2 objective. 

Error bound analysis of non-convex problems. Error bounds have been extensively studied as a fundamental tool for characterizing the local geometry of optimization problems and establishing convergence guarantees, such as linear convergence and superlinear convergence rates, for iterative algorithms [Luo and Tseng, 1993, Yue et al., 2019, Zhou and So, 2017]. While early works focused primarily on convex settings [Luo and Tseng, 1993, Zhou and So, 2017], recent studies have shown that various classes of non-convex problems also satisfy error-bound properties under structural assumptions. For example, Liu et al. [2019] showed that quadratic programs with orthogonality constraints satisfy an error-bound property, and Wang et al. [2023] showed the same result for linear programs with orthogonality constraints. Jiang and Li [2022] proved an error bound for the trust region subproblem. Recently, Chen et al. [2025] established error bounds for the regularized loss of deep matrix factorization problems. As demonstrated in these works, establishing error-bound properties generally requires dedicated analyses and advanced technical tools.

## 4 Experimental Results 

In this section, we conduct numerical experiments on synthetic datasets in Section 4.1 and real-world datasets in Section 4.2 in support of our theoretical findings. Since the main contribution of this paper is a convergence-rate theory for the MCR 2 objective, the primary quantities reported here are the objective gap and the iterate gap against the number of iterations. We also provide additional experiments in Section D to show how optimizing the MCR 2 objective relates to feature geometry and linear-probe performance. 

4.1 Convergence Rate of Gradient Ascent for MCR 2

In this subsection, we apply gradient ascent (GA) for solving Problem (3). The problem settings are as follows: We set the number of classes K = 4 and the number of data samples Nk = 200 for each k ∈ [K], and vary the data dimension d ∈ { 64 , 128 , 256 }. Moreover, we set the regularization parameter λ = 0 .1 and the coding precision ϵ = 0 .5. For the implementation of GA, the iterate is initialized using a standard Gaussian random distribution and adopts a constant step size of 0.1. To evaluate the convergence rate of GA in terms of function values (resp., iterate gap), we plot 

F (Z∗) − F (Zt) (resp., ∥Zt − Z∗∥F ) against the number of iterations. Here, Zt denotes the t-th iterate and Z∗ denotes the last iterate generated by GA, which serves as a surrogate for a local maximizer. 5 It is observed that GA with random initialization converges to a local maximizer at a linear rate in terms of both the function value gap and the iterate distance. This is consistent with the linear convergence guarantee established in Theorem 2, which in turn provides further numerical evidence supporting the error-bound condition in Theorem 1. This controlled synthetic experiment is designed to isolate the local linear regime predicted by the theory; the resulting trajectories are shown in Figure 1. 

4.2 Convergence Rate of Training Deep Networks via MCR 2

We adopt a three-layer MLP with ReLU activations and optimize the MCR 2 objective using the AdamW optimizer equipped with a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler Loshchilov and Hutter [2019]. ## 5 Conclusions 

In this work, we studied the optimization geometry of the MCR 2 problem by showing that it satisfies an error-bound property in a neighborhood of its local maximizers. This property directly implies key regularity conditions, including the PŁ inequality and quadratic growth, and allows us to establish that first-order methods converge linearly to a local maximizer. Our experimental results on synthetic and real-world datasets strongly supported our theoretical results. One future direction is to extend our analysis of Problem (3) to the constrained MCR 2 problem studied in Yu et al. [2020], which imposes a norm constraint on the features instead of using a regularizer, as well as to the sparse MCR 2

problem in Yu et al. [2024a], which considers a sparsity-inducing regularization on the features. Another promising direction is to theoretically analyze Problem (3) when the feature representations are parameterized by deep neural networks, as explored empirically in Section 4.1. Such an analysis could provide insights into the interplay between the network architecture and the optimization landscape.

## D Extension Experiments 

Section 4.2 studies the qualitative two-stage convergence behavior in a deterministic full-batch setting. Here we use a more practical minibatch training setup to examine whether progress on the MCR 2

objective remains aligned with representation quality. This experiment is complementary to the convergence plots in the main text: it tracks the objective together with linear-probe accuracy and class-mean geometry along training. 

Setup. We conduct supervised MCR 2 training on MNIST using a CNN encoder with a 64 -dimensional normalized feature output. The network is trained for 10 epochs with batch size 

512 , and we compare three optimizers: AdaGrad , RMSProp , and AdamW . The learning rates are set to 0.01 for AdaGrad and 0.001 for both RMSProp and AdamW, with weight decay 10 −4; a cosine annealing scheduler is applied throughout training. To ensure a fair comparison, all optimizers use the same model initialization and the same minibatch order. 

Objective value and linear-probe accuracy. During training, we record the MCR 2 objective value and linear-probe test accuracy. The trajectory in Figure 3 shows that optimizers that improve the MCR 2 objective more quickly also tend to obtain stronger downstream linear-probe accuracy. Table 1 reports the same trend at selected iterations. All methods start from the same initialization with identical accuracy, but AdamW consistently attains both a larger objective value and higher linear-probe accuracy during training. 

Feature separability. To examine feature separability, we compute cosine-similarity heatmaps of class-mean feature vectors using up to 5000 randomly selected training samples, where each class mean is obtained by averaging normalized features within that class. We plot these heatmaps at selected iterations to visualize how the class structure evolves during optimization in Figure 4. As optimization proceeds, the off-diagonal entries become smaller and the heatmap becomes closer to diagonal, indicating that class-mean features become more separated. This is consistent with the local maximizer structure in Fact 1(ii), where different class subspaces are orthogonal. 25 (a) Objective value vs. iteration. (b) Linear-probe accuracy vs. iteration.

Reproducibility, variability, and compute. All experiments use standard public datasets. The deep-network convergence experiments in Section 4.2 are run in a deterministic full-batch setting to isolate the local linear regime.
