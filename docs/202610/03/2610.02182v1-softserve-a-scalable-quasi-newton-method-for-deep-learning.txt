ABSTRACT 

Quasi-Newton (QN) methods have long been among the most effective methods for large-scale unconstrained convex optimization. Two obstacles have limited their use in deep learning: non-convexity and enormous parameter sizes. We introduce SOFT SERVE , a family of QN methods designed to overcome these obstacles without line searches or ad hoc curvature corrections. S OFT SERVE derives positive-definite curvature estimates from the variational objective of Berglund et al. (2025), even in the presence of negative curvature. We develop diagonal and Kronecker-factored variants that preserve positive definiteness by construction and scale to massive neural networks. Finally, S OFT SERVE relies on the stable coupled Newton–Schulz iteration for the required matrix operations, replacing costly matrix decompositions with GPU-friendly matrix multiplications. S OFT SERVE excels on problems that are severely ill-conditioned, including tasks such as recurrent networks, deep autoencoders, physics-informed neural networks, and a 136M-parameter physics-informed diffusion model, often achieving lower losses than established baselines including Adam, Muon, and SOAP. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Quasi-Newton methods, such as the celebrated BFGS method (Broyden, 1970; Fletcher, 1970; Goldfarb, 1970; Shanno, 1970), are among the most effective methods for large-scale, convex deterministic optimization. Deep learning has led to renewed interest in adapting these methods to train large networks (Goldfarb et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022). However, applying these methods to deep learning requires curvature estimates that can accommodate non-convex objectives and gradient noise while remaining computationally efficient. To see how curvature enters the optimization update, consider a generic second-order method. For parameters x ∈ RD , second-order methods typically take steps of the form 

x+ = x − γHg. 

Here x+ is the updated parameter vector, and γ > 0 is the step size. The vector g ∈ RD is the (stochastic) gradient at x, and the matrix H ∈ RD×D is an estimate of the inverse Hessian. Non-convexity of the objective poses a fundamental challenge for second-order optimization methods because the true Hessian could have negative eigenvalues. If the estimate H has a negative eigenvalue, then the update direction −Hg could be an ascent direction, which would increase the loss function. This is one of the reasons why first-order methods are often preferred in deep learning: one can always guarantee descent (in expectation). For a second-order method to be applicable in deep learning, the estimate H must always be positive definite, even in the presence of directions of negative curvature. For quasi-Newton methods, such as BFGS, maintaining positive definiteness is at odds with the main tool used to measure curvature, namely the secant equation . In the presence of negative curvature, enforcing the secant equation can inadvertently force the Hessian estimate to become indefinite, as we will show in Section 2. The standard remedy is to use sophisticated line search procedures to

But these line searches require access to the full batch loss, which is too costly and not feasible in the stochastic setting of deep learning. Beyond the challenges arising from non-convexity and stochastic objectives, the sheer scale of modern deep learning models makes storing and updating dense curvature estimates prohibitively expensive. A standard approach for reducing this cost is the limited memory approach of L-BFGS (Liu & Nocedal, 1989), which typically uses only 10 pairs of parameter and gradient differences to implicitly build the estimate H. But storing 10 pairs of parameters and gradients would require storing 10 copies of our model and 10 copies of the gradient, increasing the memory storage by 20-fold. This is infeasible for all but small models. To scale to modern deep learning, it is common to approximate the matrix H by a diagonal ma-trix (Duchi et al., 2011; Tieleman & Hinton, 2012; Kingma & Ba, 2015) or a Kronecker-structured ma-trix (Martens & Grosse, 2015; Gupta et al., 2018). For quasi-Newton optimization, while Kronecker-structured updates have been proposed for applying BFGS to deep learning (Goldfarb et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022), this approach still needs to be applied with mechanisms for handling non-convexity and stochasticity, limiting its applicability. A promising new direction is to reconsider how curvature enters the quasi-Newton update. Recently, Berglund et al. (2025) show that by replacing the secant equation with a soft secant penalty we can ensure that our Hessian estimate is positive definite, even in the presence of negative curvature. But Berglund et al. (2025) only consider dense estimates, making their approach infeasible for deep learning. We introduce S OFT SERVE (Soft Secant Equation Regularized with Variational Entropy), a quasi-Newton framework that combines the flexibility of a soft secant constraint (Li, 2019; Berglund et al., 2025) with the computational advantages of a structured matrix approximation of the inverse Hessian. Optimizing within diagonal and Kronecker-structured families yields novel updates that preserve positive definiteness, without full batch evaluations or line searches. We develop efficient algorithms for computing these updates using modern GPU-friendly numerical linear algebra methods, such as variants of Newton-Schulz (Amsel et al., 2026; Higham, 2008), allowing S OFT SERVE to scale to large neural networks. We then demonstrate the utility of S OFT SERVE on several deep learning tasks where curvature information is fundamental—such as severely ill-conditioned tasks—including recurrent networks, autoencoders, and physics-informed models (such as a 136M-parameter diffusion model). On these tasks we find that S OFT SERVE often outperforms modern methods such as Adam, Muon, and SOAP. A Python implementation of S OFT SERVE is available at https://github.com/joohwanko/ SoftServe .

5 RELATED WORK 

Stochastic and structured Quasi-Newton. Classical QN methods enforce the secant equation while changing the metric as little as possible (Broyden, 1970; Fletcher, 1970; Goldfarb, 1970; Shanno, 1970; Dennis & Mor´ e, 1977; Nocedal & Wright, 2006). Stochastic variants keep a dense or limited-memory metric (Liu & Nocedal, 1989) and protect it from bad pairs by: using damping (Schraudolph et al., 2007), curvature pairs from Hessian-vector products on large batches (Byrd et al., 2016), damped updates (Wang et al., 2017), overlapping batches (Berahas et al., 2016), diagonal rescaling (Bordes et al., 2009), or a secant penalty (Irwin & Haber, 2023). K-BFGS (Goldfarb et al., 2020) uses a per-layer Kronecker factorization of BFGS, and relies on damping for a resulting positive definite curvature estimate. 

Matrix-aware methods. There are now a growing number of methods which are specialized to the matrix parameters in linear layers. K-FAC (Martens & Grosse, 2015), Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018), and SOAP (Vyas et al., 2025) all make use of Kronecker factorizations to form a preconditioner. Muon (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025) is a steepest descent method in the matrix spectral norm. 

Related variational objectives. With the mean held fixed, the covariance subproblem in batch-and-match variational inference (Cai et al., 2024) has the same form as the soft QN objective in Eq. 5, which is where the closed form in Eq. 13 originates (see Section A.3 for details). The least squares secant penalty within the soft QN objective (Eq. 5) has also been used to design preconditioned variants of SGD called PSGD in (Li, 2018; Pooladzandi & Li, 2024). 6 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate S OFT SERVE on recurrent sequence learning, image reconstruction, and physics-informed learning, from a small recurrent network to a 136M-parameter diffusion model. We test whether the structured inverse Hessian estimates improve training over first-order and other preconditioned methods. We compare training losses at equal gradient-evaluation budgets, and report prediction errors and wall-clock comparisons in Appendix E. 

Comparison protocol. Our baselines are Adam, Muon, SOAP, K-FAC, and K-BFGS(L), with L-BFGS also included for deterministic training. To assess the contribution of the learned inverse Hessian, we include S OFT SERVE -I: the normalized momentum method Eq. 14 obtained by fixing 

H = I. We tune each method’s learning rate separately and evaluate the selected configurations on three fresh seeds. All S OFT SERVE runs refresh the inverse Hessian estimate every K = 10 updates. Gradient counts include the additional evaluations used for secant pairs, line searches, and loss balancing. In stochastic training, S OFT SERVE uses one extra gradient evaluation every K updates to form a secant pair on the same batch, see detailed pseudo-code in Algorithm D.1. Task-specific settings and learning-rate sweeps are given in Appendix E. 6.1 RNN A DDING 

The Adding problem tests a recurrent network’s ability to retain information over a long sequence. Each input contains 100 numbers sampled uniformly from [0 , 1] and markers identifying two positions. The network reads the full sequence and predicts the sum of the two marked numbers. We train a tanh RNN with 16 hidden units by minimizing mean squared error (MSE) on batches of 128 newly sampled sequences. Although the network is small, repeated applications of the recurrent map can cause gradients to vanish or explode (Pascanu et al., 2013). The full loss Hessian in this example is numerically nearly singular at initialization across four seeds (Appendix E.2). This makes Adding an interesting ill-conditioned small-scale test. Indeed, in Figure 1 we see that preconditioning was necessary to reach a reasonable loss, with S OFT SERVE -Kron reaching by far the lowest training loss (Figure 1, left), outperforming our baselines on all three seeds. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) approximate solutions to partial differential equations (PDEs) by mapping space–time coordinates to solution values. They are trained by penalizing the PDE residual and errors in the initial and boundary conditions. Depending on the differential operator, this loss can be very ill-conditioned, motivating the use of second-order optimization (Rathore et al., 2024). We evaluate S OFT SERVE on both small feed-forward PINNs and the larger residual architecture PirateNet (Wang et al., 2024). 

Small PINNs. We use the Wave, Convection, and Reaction problems of Rathore et al. (2024). Each network has three hidden layers of width 200 and 81,201 parameters. Training minimizes the sum of the mean squared residuals of the governing PDE and its associated initial and boundary conditions. We either fix the sampled training coordinates (collocation points) or resample them at each update. This gives six settings across the three equations. SOFT SERVE -Kron attains the lowest final training objective in five of the six settings, including all three with resampled points (Figure 2). On deterministic Wave, Adam and Adam+L-BFGS attain lower final losses, with Adam+L-BFGS achieving the lowest. Adam+L-BFGS, used by Rathore et al. (2024), runs Adam for the first 11,000 parameter updates, followed by L-BFGS within the remaining gradient-evaluation budget. Appendix E.4 gives solution errors and repeats the comparison in double precision. For our remaining experiments with larger models below, we restrict the baselines to Adam, Muon, and SOAP, based on their scalability, and performance in the preceding experiments. 

PirateNet. PirateNet is a PINN architecture that combines fully connected layers with skip con-nections to make deeper networks easier to train. We use a 0.73M-parameter model to solve the Allen–Cahn and Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equations. Training uses 32,768 collocation points per update and adaptive weights for the loss terms. Since these weights differ across optimizers, we compare the unweighted sum of PDE and initial-condition MSE on the same fixed points. Against Adam, Muon, and SOAP, S OFT SERVE -Kron is competitive with Muon on Allen–Cahn and reaches the lowest mean loss on KdV (Figure 3, left and middle). 7 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We introduce S OFT SERVE , a quasi-Newton approach for deep learning. S OFT SERVE considers structured Hessian approximations that are always positive definite, GPU-friendly Newton–Schulz approximations, allowing it to scale to massive neural networks. Our experiments highlight the value of curvature on several ill-conditioned tasks, including recurrent networks, autoencoders, and physics-informed models, including the 136M-parameter PIDM. Many future directions remain. For instance, currently we use a fixed λ within each training run. Developing adaptive λ would further optimize performance. Finally, characterizing convergence guarantees under realistic settings remains an open problem to study. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

D.1 TRAINING WITH SOFT SERVE 

We provide more of the details of how our S OFT SERVE methods are implemented. In particular, this section will explain the detailed pseudo code of S OFT SERVE -Kron and S OFT SERVE -Diag is in Algorithm D.1 and Algorithm D.2, respectively. SOFT SERVE -Kron is a method for updating matrix parameters, where we will use X ∈ Rn×m to denote the matrix parameters of a given layer. Whereas S OFT SERVE -Diag is applicable to parameters of any shape, and thus in Algorithm D.2, we represent the given parameters as a vector x ∈ Rd. If this corresponds to a linear layer, then d = nm , where n × m are the dimensions of the linear layer. To make S OFT SERVE practical, we will make use of some now-standard practices in deep learning, including the use of momentum, updating the approximate Hessian every so often to amortize costs, and taking care to properly scale the update directions. We address each of these considerations in turn. To estimate the gradient for the quasi-Newton step, we will use momentum estimate which in matrix form is updated according to lines (6–7) in Algorithm D.1, and in vector form is updated according to lines (6–7) in Algorithm D.2. We also incorporate an additional Nesterov extrapolation or the debiasing trick Kingma & Ba (2015) to form the final momentum estimate. Restricting the Hessian approximation to a diagonal or Kronecker factor greatly reduces the costs as compared to the dense Soft QN method. For instance, for a n × m linear layer, the state, application, and refresh costs of S OFT SERVE are given in Table D.1. D.3 REPLAY AND GRADIENT ACCOUNTING 

A replay key stores every random choice that affects fζt : the minibatch indices, augmentation, dropout masks, and any other sampled loss input. Replaying the key at both endpoints removes sampling mismatch from yt. The replayed gradient is used only for the refresh; it does not update momentum, the learning-rate schedule, or the parameters. For N parameter steps, Algorithm D.1 evaluates N ordinary gradients and ⌊(N − 1) /K ⌋ replayed ones, and we count both. With decoupled weight decay, the displacement st is the actual parameter displacement, including the decay. A checkpoint taken inside an interval must retain the momentum, the metric state, the interval start, the cached raw gradient, and the replay key.

Task and data. We solve the Allen–Cahn and Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equations on (x, t ) ∈

[−1, 1] × [0 , 1] , where u(x, t ) is the scalar solution. 

Allen–Cahn. 

∂u ∂t − 10 −4 ∂2u∂x 2 + 5 u3 − 5u = 0 ,u(x, 0) = x2 cos( πx ).

KdV. 

∂u ∂t + u ∂u ∂x + 0 .022 2 ∂3u∂x 3 = 0 ,u(x, 0) = cos( πx ).

Both problems use periodic spatial boundaries. Reference solutions for evaluation are taken from the 

jaxpi repository. 1

Model and objective. We use PirateNet (Wang et al., 2024), a residual PINN with three width-256 blocks, tanh activations, and approximately 0.73M parameters. The network uses periodic spatial features, and its final layer is initialized by least squares against the initial condition. Following Wang et al. (2025), training minimizes a weighted combination of PDE residual and initial-condition MSEs. Causal weighting prioritizes earlier times, while gradient-norm balancing adjusts the relative weights of the loss terms. These weights are treated as constants during differentiation and held fixed between the two secant evaluations. 

Training and tuning. Each update samples 32,768 uniform collocation points. We compare methods at 300,600 gradient evaluations for Allen–Cahn and 100,200 for KdV, counting both secant replay and loss-balancing gradients. Learning rates use a 5,000-update warmup followed by exponential decay. Matrix methods use β1 = 0 .995 and Adam on the remaining parameters. Momentum settings are fixed during tuning. Learning-rate sweeps run for 300,000 updates on Allen–Cahn and 100,000 on KdV, with selection based on unweighted validation loss (Figure E.13). The selected λ values for S OFT SERVE -Kron are 

λ = 9999 for Allen–Cahn and λ = 999 for KdV. 

Evaluation. Although all optimizers use the same weighting rules, their training weights evolve differently. We therefore compare the unweighted sum of PDE and initial-condition MSEs on common fixed points. Figure E.14 shows this loss against parameter updates and wall-clock time. Figure E.15 reports relative L2 solution errors on the reference grid. Solution errors are not used for learning-rate selection. All curves stop at the same gradient budgets as the main figures, so SOFT SERVE -Kron takes fewer parameter updates because of replay.

Task and data. We consider structural design using the mechanics dataset of Bastek et al. (2025), which contains 30,000 optimized structures. Given supports, applied loads, and a material budget, the model generates a material-density field and two displacement fields. We retain the released 65 × 65 

field representation and 64 × 64 finite-element grid. 

Model and objective. We use the released 136M-parameter U-Net, consisting of residual convolu-tion blocks and attention layers. It predicts the two displacement fields and the material density, with a sigmoid output for density. Let z0 contain the target fields and c the design conditions. At diffusion timestep t, we construct a noisy input 

zt = √¯αtz0 + √1 − ¯αtε, ε ∼ N (0 , I ),

where ¯αt is the cumulative signal-retention factor. The network predicts the clean fields as bz0 =

fθ (zt, t, c ). The training objective combines reconstruction and physics-based terms: 

L(θ) = Ewt MSE( bz0, z 0) + ℓt(req ) + 10 ℓt(rvol ) + 10 −4C . (E.3) The expectation is over training examples, diffusion timesteps, and noise. The equilibrium residual is 

req = K(bρ)bu − f, 

after applying boundary conditions, where K(bρ) is the stiffness matrix determined by the predicted density, bu is the predicted displacement, and f is the applied load. The volume residual rvol measures deviation from the material budget. Compliance C measures structural flexibility under the load. We retain the released denoising weights wt and Gaussian residual penalties ℓt, including their diffusion-dependent variances, for all optimizers. 

Training and tuning. We use batches of 16, 100 diffusion timesteps, constant learning rates, gradient clipping at 1, and no weight decay. An exponential moving average (EMA) of the parameters uses decay 0.99 after the first 1,000 updates. Only learning rates are tuned. Each method initially tests five rates for 10,000 updates, extending the grid when needed. Selection uses the EMA validation objective on 128 fixed examples with fixed noise (Figure E.16). Final runs use a budget of 100,000 gradient evaluations. Momentum and λ remain fixed. Muon uses β1 = 0 .95 with Nesterov; Adam, SOAP, and S OFT SERVE -Kron use β1 = 0 .9. Adam and SOAP use β2 = 0 .999 . S OFT SERVE -Kron uses λ = 9 without Nesterov. Matrix methods use the same convolution-kernel partitions, with blocks of at most 

256 × 256 , and Adam at learning rate 10 −4 on the remaining parameters. 

Evaluation. We evaluate the training objective on 32 fixed examples with fixed noise using the current, unaveraged parameters. Validation uses the EMA parameters on 128 separate fixed ex-amples. Figure E.17 shows both objectives against gradient evaluations, parameter updates, and wall-clock time. All curves stop at 100,000 gradient evaluations. Task and data. We train a language model on 200M tokens from FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024). The task is to predict each token from its preceding context. 

Model and objective. We use a 16.17M-parameter GPT-style model (Radford et al., 2019) with four layers, four attention heads, hidden width 256, and context length 512. We minimize next-token cross-entropy, reported as negative log-likelihood (NLL) per token. 

Training and tuning. Each update uses 64 sequences, giving 32,768 tokens per full update. The 200M-token budget requires 6,104 updates, with a smaller final batch. Kron additionally uses 610 same-batch gradient replays. The learning rate warms up over the first 2% of training, remains constant, then decays by cosine to one tenth of its peak over the final 10%. We use no dropout, weight decay, or gradient clipping. We tune learning rates on seed 0 using five-point sweeps at the full 200M-token budget, selecting by validation NLL. We first tune AdamW, then use its selected peak learning rate of 0.003 for the AdamW updates outside the matrix methods. Muon, SOAP, and Kron update the same attention and MLP weight matrices, with AdamW on the remaining parameters. The selected peak learning rates are 0.003 for Muon and SOAP, and 0.01 for Kron. Kron uses λ = 99 , β1 = 0 .99 , K = 10 , and no Nesterov. Muon uses β1 = 0 .9 with Nesterov. SOAP uses betas (0 .95 , 0.95) and refreshes its preconditioner every 10 updates. AdamW uses betas 

(0 .9, 0.95) , both as a baseline and for the remaining parameters of the matrix methods. Momentum, Nesterov, and λ are fixed throughout tuning. 

Evaluation. We evaluate the selected configurations on fresh seeds 1–3 and report their mean NLL. Final training NLL uses one million training tokens; learning-rate selection and final validation NLL use five million held-out tokens.
