## ABSTRACT 

Sheaf Neural Networks generalize scalar-weighted message passing by replacing scalar edge weights with linear transport maps between local feature spaces. Yet the role of this matrix-valued transport is entangled with the broader sheaf-diffusion construction. We isolate the transport primitive through quiver representations and establish a direct connection with multi-head attention. Treating attention heads as coordinates of a local transport space reveals that standard multi-head attention implements diagonal edge maps: along each directed interaction, a source head can contribute only to the corresponding receiver head. Allowing off-diagonal entries instead enables edge-conditioned communication across heads before neighbor-hood aggregation. We show that this operation cannot, in general, be absorbed into a single shared linear map applied after aggregation. Building on this characteri-zation, we introduce Topological Attention (Top-A), a multi-head attention that learns edge-dependent off-diagonal routes while preserving the original same-head paths and exactly recovering vanilla attention when the additional routing vanishes. We evaluate Top-A on relational reasoning, heterogeneous graph learning, and al-gorithmic reasoning, including out-of-distribution generalization, with heterophilic node classification as a contrast setting. The results show that cross-head transport is most useful when the task benefits from interaction-dependent transformations, while heterophily alone provides no systematic advantage. These findings identify edge-conditioned cross-head communication as a distinct computational primitive of matrix-valued transport. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) (Scarselli et al., 2009; Micheli, 2009; Defferrard et al., 2016; Gilmer et al., 2017; Kipf & Welling, 2017) learn by propagating information along graph edges, with most message-passing architectures using scalar weights to modulate neighboring representations. Sheaf Neural Networks (SNNs) (Hansen & Gebhart, 2020; Bodnar et al., 2022) generalize this paradigm by equipping edges with linear transport operators between local feature spaces, allowing neighboring representations to be transformed rather than merely reweighted. Introduced through cellular sheaf theory (Hansen & Ghrist, 2019), this richer form of local transport was motivated by phenomena such as heterophily (Luan et al., 2024) and oversmoothing (Cai & Wang, 2020; Oono & Suzuki, 2020; Rusch et al., 2023), and has since inspired a growing family of architectures. Yet, the classical sheaf construction couples several components: restriction maps connect node and edge spaces, their compositions induce transport between neighboring nodes, and the resulting transformations are assembled through the sheaf Laplacian. Consequently, the specific computational capability contributed by richer local transport remains unclear. While the original motivation for SNNs is rooted in sheaf diffusion and topology, recent architectures increasingly learn edge-dependent matrix

Moreover, recent evidence questions whether gains under heterophily and oversmoothing arise specifically from the learned sheaf structure (Hernandez Caralt et al., 2026; Fiorini et al., 2026). This motivates a more fundamental question: what is actually useful about sheaf transport? We address it by isolating the local transformation induced by learned sheaf structure. Rather than asking when SNNs outperform conventional GNNs, we ask which additional form of message passing matrix-valued transport enables and how it differs from standard graph propagation. This leads us from cellular sheaves to quiver representations (Hajij et al., 2025), where directed interactions are represented directly by linear maps. At this level, transport itself can be studied independently of the restriction-map factorization and diffusion dynamics of classical SNNs, while revealing a direct connection with multi-head attention. If attention heads are treated as coordinates of the local transport space, multi-head attention induces a diagonal map on each directed interaction: each source head contributes only to the corresponding receiver head, even though different heads may assign different weights to the same interaction. Thus, standard attention spans only the diagonal subfamily of matrix-valued head-space transport. Off-diagonal entries extend this mechanism with edge-conditioned communication across heads before neighborhood aggregation. We show that this edge-specific mixing cannot, in general, be absorbed into a shared post-aggregation projection, and therefore constitutes a distinct operation beyond standard multi-head attention. Motivated by this, we introduce Topological Attention (Top-A), which augments standard attention with edge-conditioned off-diagonal routing across heads while preserving the original same-head paths. When the routing vanishes, Top-A reduces exactly to vanilla attention, providing a controlled way to study when cross-head transport is useful independently of other architectural changes. We evaluate this inductive bias on algorithmic reasoning with CLRS (Veliˇ ckovi´ c et al., 2022), relational reasoning with STaR (Khalid & Schockaert, 2025), heterogeneous graph learning (Harper & Konstan, 2015), and, as a contrast setting, classical heterophilic node classification (Platonov et al., 2023). Together, these benchmarks probe edge-specific computation across relational composition, directed heterogeneous interactions, and algorithmic size generalization, while isolating whether heterophily alone is sufficient to make cross-head transport beneficial. Our main contributions are: (i) We use quiver representations to disentangle matrix-valued edge transport from the broader sheaf-diffusion construction, establishing a direct connection to multi-head attention. (ii) We characterize matrix-valued transport as edge-conditioned communication across attention heads and prove that, in general, it cannot be reduced to a shared post-aggregation projection. (iii) We introduce Top-A, an architectural extension implementing this transport primitive, and evaluate it across relational, heterogeneous, and algorithmic reasoning, including compositional-and size-generalization regimes.

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate the proposed head-space transport across complementary computational settings. In Section 5.1, a controlled relational reasoning benchmark tests whether edge-conditioned cross-head transport improves relation classification, including at reasoning depths beyond those seen during training. In Section 5.2, a real-world heterogeneous graph task assesses whether communication across head subspaces provides a useful inductive bias for integrating information from nodes and relations with distinct semantics. In Section 5.3, we look into algorithmic reasoning tasks, investigating which algorithmic procedures benefit from the addedrouting capacity of Top-A and whether these gains extend to larger problem instances. As a contrast setting, Appendix C.4 evaluates whether cross-head transport also benefits standard heterophilic node-classification benchmarks. Task details, including hyperparameter settings and search spaces, are reported in Appendices C.1, C.2, and C.3. 

Attention Backbones. Because Top-A is agnostic to the underlying attention mechanism, we instantiate it with GATv2 (Brody et al., 2022) and a Graph Transformer (GT) (Shi et al., 2021), comparing each backbone with its corresponding Top-A variants. In both architectures, the interaction representation ξe in Equation (15) is constructed from quantities already available to the respective attention mechanism. For GATv2, ξe uses the same directed sender–receiver context as attention computation; for GT, it combines the receiver query and the edge-conditioned sender key. The resulting routing matrix Ωe remains specific to each directed interaction, allowing distinct edges to induce distinct cross-head transformations. ## 6 CONCLUSION 

We revisited matrix-valued transport as a principled generalization of multi-head attention beyond its diagonal structure in head space. By allowing off-diagonal maps, edges can coordinate information flow across heads before aggregation. This perspective led to Top-A, a direct realization of the resulting transport primitive that retains standard attention as a special case. Across relational, heterogeneous, and algorithmic reasoning tasks, this richer routing mechanism consistently provides a useful inductive bias. More broadly, our results establish a direct connection between sheaf transport and attention through a simple yet powerful principle: edges should determine not only how much information is transmitted, but also how it is routed across heads.

STaR (Khalid & Schockaert, 2025) is a relational reasoning benchmark that provides a controlled setting in which edge-dependent transformations are themselves part of the computation. Each instance consists of an edge-labelled graph together with a query pair of nodes, and the task is to infer the relation holding between them. STaR contains two relational domains, each comprising 57,600 training instances and 153,600 test instances: RCC-8, with eight qualitative spatial relations, and Allen’s Interval Algebra (IA), with thirteen temporal relations. In both domains, the relation carried by an edge determines not only whether information is relevant, but also how the current relational state should be transformed before propagation. Unlike conventional single-path reasoning benchmarks, STaR requires both composing relations along individual paths and integrating the partial, potentially disjunctive information provided by multiple paths. Problem difficulty is controlled by the path length k and the number of paths b connecting the queried nodes. Models are trained on graphs with 

k ∈ { 2, 3, 4} and b ∈ { 1, 2, 3}, and evaluated at k ∈ { 2, 3, . . . , 15 } while keeping b ∈ { 1, 2, 3}. This allows us to evaluate systematic generalization to reasoning depths beyond those observed during training while keeping the number of paths within the training range. Relation labels are supplied as edge information to both the attention backbone and the Top-A routing mechanism, so that the comparison isolates the additional capacity provided by cross-head transport rather than access to relational information. Within each backbone family, the baseline 17 Preprint and Top-A variant share the same input representation, backbone architecture, prediction head, optimization procedure, and tuning budget. As throughout our experiments, the routing branch is zero-initialized, so that each Top-A model starts from the same function as its corresponding attention baseline. Performance is measured by query-relation classification accuracy across the different (k, b )

configurations. We otherwise follow the experimental setting of Khalid & Schockaert (2025). 

Hyperparameters and training protocol. All STaR experiments use a fixed hyperparameter configuration. Both GATv2 and the GT use a hidden dimension of 64, four attention heads, and 15 recurrent message-passing rounds with shared processor weights. The Top-A variant has a hidden dimension of 64 and a transport scale of 1. Training uses AdamW with learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −5, and batch size 128. Attention dropout is disabled, while residual dropout is set to 0.1.Models train for at most 40 epochs, with early stopping after eight epochs without improvement in validation accuracy; the checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy is retained. For both RCC-8 and Interval Algebra, the released training mixture covers b ∈ { 1, 2, 3} and 

k ∈ { 2, 3, 4}. An 80/20 training–validation split is stratified jointly by target relation and (b, k )

configuration. Each experiment uses three training seeds. Within each architecture, the baseline and Top-A variants share the training configuration, initial shared parameters, minibatch order, and dropout randomness. Dataset, task, and model configuration. MovieLens-100K (Harper & Konstan, 2015) provides a real-world heterogeneous setting in which information is exchanged between two semantically distinct node types. We represent the dataset as a bipartite graph containing 943 users, described by 24-dimensional demographic features, and 1,682 movies, described by 18-dimensional genre features. Each observed rating induces two directed interactions, rates (u → m) and rated-by (m → u), which exchange the roles of sender and receiver while connecting the same user–movie pair. We formulate the task as binary user–movie link prediction. We use the official 80k/20k rating split for training and test positive edges, respectively, and hold out 10% of the training ratings as a fixed validation set. Validation edges are removed from the message-passing graph. Negative examples are sampled uniformly from unobserved user–movie pairs using a 1:1 positive-to-negative ratio, with validation and test negatives fixed across runs. Type-specific input encoders map user and movie attributes to a shared 64-dimensional representation, to which we add a learned node-type embedding. Relation type is represented by a one-hot edge feature and is available to both the attention mechanism and the Top-A routing generator. We evaluate GATv2 and a sparse GT, together with their corresponding Top-A variants. All architectures use H = 4 attention heads, two message-passing layers, and a dot-product link-prediction decoder. For each backbone family, the baseline and Top-A variant share the same input encoders, backbone architecture, decoder, and training protocol. Hyperparameters are selected on validation performance using tuning seeds that are disjoint from the final evaluation seeds. The learning-rate search is shared across all models, while the Top-A variants additionally select the transport scale λ, as detailed below. Final results are evaluated over ten seeds that are disjoint from those used for hyperparameter selection, and test AUROC and AUPR are measured at the best-validation epoch. For each evaluation seed, the baseline and Top-A variant use the same initialization for all shared parameters, while the routing generator is zero-initialized. Consequently, the paired models implement the same function before optimization.

Setup. We evaluate on the seven benchmarks of Platonov et al. (2023), using the ten official train/val-idation/test splits provided with each dataset. The multi-class datasets ( roman-empire , amazon-ratings ,

squirrel , and chameleon ) are evaluated by accuracy, while the binary datasets ( minesweeper , tolokers ,and questions ) are evaluated by ROC-AUC. Early stopping and hyperparameter selection use the corresponding validation metric. Within each backbone family, the baseline and its Top-A variant share the same encoder, processor depth, decoder, optimization schedule, data split, and seed. Each Top-A run is initialized from the corresponding same-seed baseline, while the routing generator is zero-initialized, so that the two models implement the same function before training. All models use H = 4 attention heads and five message-passing layers, with head width 64 for GATv2 and 56 

for the GT. Parameter counts for all models are reported in Table 10. Each arm is given the same hyperparameter search over learning rate ∈ { 10 −3, 3 · 10 −4} and dropout ∈ { 0, 0.2, 0.5}, tuned on split 0 using two seeds and selected by validation performance alone. Before training, every Top-A run is verified to match its paired baseline at Ωe := 0 .

Results. As shown in Table 8, cross-head routing does not provide a systematic advantage on these heterophilic benchmarks. Across the 14 dataset–backbone comparisons, most changes are small and vary in sign across datasets and architectures. The clearest exception is the GT on tolokers , where Top-A improves ROC-AUC from 77 .89 to 84 .76 . A smaller positive effect is also observed for the GT on roman-empire (+0 .90 accuracy points), while the remaining comparisons remain close to their corresponding baselines. Overall, adding Top-A does not consistently improve performance over the corresponding attention backbone in this benchmark suite. 

What heterophily does and does not imply. These results help clarify the role of the transport inductive bias that motivates Top-A. Heterophily was one of the original settings in which matrix-valued sheaf transport was argued to be useful, since neighboring representations may need to be transformed rather than simply reweighted. By isolating a related matrix-valued transport mechanism within attention, we can ask whether this additional flexibility is itself sufficient to yield systematic gains on heterophilic graphs. The results suggest that it is not: cross-head transport provides no consistent advantage across these benchmarks. Together with the main experiments, this supports a more specific view of the inductive bias, whose usefulness appears most clearly in settings where individual interactions call for different transformations before aggregation.
