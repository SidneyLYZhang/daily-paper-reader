Abstract 

Recent work has established that power-law spectral conditions on data enable tight convergence bounds for deterministic gradient descent, resolving the conflict between classical exponential bounds and observed power-law learning curves. In this work, we extend this result to the stochastic regime of high-dimensional machine learning. We provide two main contributions: (1) We generalize the power-law spectral theory to Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), showing that the same spectral exponents govern stochastic dynamics; (2) For the fundamental case of isotropic Gaussian data, we provide a precise analysis of data shuffling, deriving exact constants that prove Single Shuffle is strictly superior to Flip-Flop and IID sampling. Our results bridge the gap between abstract spectral theory and practical stochastic training choices, offering a unified picture of how data geometry drives optimization speed. 

Keywords: Stochastic Gradient Descent, Power-Law Convergence, Spectral Analysis, Shuffling, Free Probability

1 Introduction 

Classical optimization theory predicts exponential convergence rates for gradient-based methods on strongly convex objectives, but in modern large-scale training we often observe approximate power-law learning curves instead (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Muennighoff et al., 2023). The simplest setting that exhibits this phenomenon is gradient descent on least-squares regression, with training loss function 1

1.1 Related Work Power-law spectra in optimization. The connection between spectral conditions and convergence rates has a long history (Nemirovsky and Yudin, 1983; Caponnetto and De Vito, 2007). Recent work has established tight bounds for full GD, steepest descent, heavy ball, and conjugate gradients under power-law spectral conditions (Velikanov and Yarotsky, 2024). Our work extends this framework to stochastic methods. 

Shuffled SGD. The benefits of shuffling over IID sampling have been studied extensively (Bottou, 2009; Recht and Ré, 2012; Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2019; Mishchenko et al., 2020; Safran and Shamir, 2020). Prior results typically provide bounds showing shuffling is no worse (and sometimes better) than IID, but do not give exact constants. Our free-probability approach yields sharp constants for the Gaussian case.

Remark 1 (Over- and under-parameterization, m̸ = d) Several qualitative features de-pend on the aspect ratio γ = m/d .

• In the underdetermined setting, m < d , X has a nontrivial null space. For least squares, components of e = w − w∗ in ker( X) are invisible to the updates and remain unchanged. Consequently, training loss can decay to 0 while test loss plateaus at ∥Πker( X)e0∥2.

• The near-critical setting of m/d ≈ 1 is of particular interest. The power-law regime is longest when the limiting spectrum touches 0 with a power-law density (our main assumption). If there is a small but positive left edge λmin > 0, then the power law persists only up to times n ≪ 1/λ min , after which exponential decay takes over. 

2.1 Background: The Spectral Condition 

Define the (empirical) Gram/Hessian matrix 

H := 1

and let λmax = λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ · · · ≥ λd ≥ 0 denote its eigenvalues. Following Velikanov and Yarotsky (2024), we work with the eigenvalue CDF ρ on [0 , λ max ] (equivalently, the empirical spectral distribution). Our main assumption is a power-law bound on the cumulative spectral measure near the left edge: 8Assumption 2 (Power-Law Spectral Condition) There exist constants Q > 0 and β > 

0 such that 

ρ(( λmin , λ ]) ≍ Q(λ − λmin )β , λ near λmin . (5) 

When λmin = 0 (the “critically determined” case), this simplifies to ρ((0 , λ ]) ≤ Qλ β .

This condition captures how “information-dense” the low-eigenvalue region is: smaller β

means more spectral mass near the edge, leading to slower convergence. In some cases, this spectral condition is already known to hold theoretically for a data-generation process of interest: for isotropic Gaussian data in the m = d regime, the Marchenko-Pastur law gives λmin = 0 and β = 1 /2; for neural tangent kernels on d-dimensional inputs with ReLU activations, one typically has β = 1 /(d + 1) (Jacot et al., 2018). 

Remark 3 (Finite-d shifted edges) In experiments one often fits a shifted edge law 

F (λ) − F (λ0) ≈ Q(λ − λ0)β with λ0 slightly below the smallest observed eigenvalue. When 

β ≳ 1 and/or λ0 is not tiny, the finite-d gap xmin := λmin − λ0 can be non-negligible on the time scales of interest and the naive replacement xmin = 0 can noticeably mispredict the constant. Appendix A, Proposition 15 gives a simple “gap+atom” correction (upper incomplete Gamma) that improves finite-d accuracy and interpolates between the power-law and minimum-eigenvalue regimes.

2.4 Why Power-Law Spectra Are Ubiquitous 

Assumption 2 covers a remarkably broad range of practical data, as has been observed repeatedly in the literature: 1. Heavy-tailed SGD iterates: Gürbüzbalaban, Şimşekli, and Zhu (Gurbuzbalaban et al., 2021) showed that the stationary distribution of SGD iterates (even on quadratic objectives, with constant stepsize and mini-batch noise) is heavy-tailed , with a tail index determined by stepsize, batch size, and curvature. Combined with multiplicative updates, this yields power-law tails in weights/features and thus in Gram spectra. 2. Heavy-tailed sample covariances: Belinschi, Dembo, and Guionnet (Belinschi et al., 2009) proved that for heavy-tailed samples (entries in the domain of attraction of an 

α-stable law, 0 < α < 2), the empirical spectral distribution of sample covariance matrices converges to a deterministic heavy-tailed limit law, not Marchenko–Pastur. 3. Neural tangent kernels: Belfer et al. (Belfer et al., 2024) showed that for wide ReLU networks, the Neural Tangent Kernel is a zonal kernel whose Mercer spectrum on the sphere has polynomial decay: eigenvalues at spherical-harmonic degree k scale like k−d,yielding a power-law. 4. Stationary signals and 1/f noise: Szegő’s theorem (and block-Toeplitz extensions (Widom, 1974)) implies that for stationary signals/images, large Toeplitz covariance matrices have eigenvalues that asymptotically sample the power spectral density ; if the PSD behaves like |ω|−κ near 0 (a 1/f κ law), then the eigenvalue profile obeys a power law. 5. Universal edge behavior: Erdős, Krüger, and Schröder (cusp universality) proved that for broad random-matrix classes, spectral edge exponents are universal: at a regular/soft edge the density vanishes like x1/2 (giving β = 1 /2); at a cusp it vanishes like |x − x0|1/3

(giving β = 1 /3) (Erdős et al., 2020). 11 6. Heavy-tailed spectral extremes: Auffinger, Ben Arous, and Péché established that for heavy-tailed random matrices, the largest eigenvalues follow a Poisson point process with Fréchet (power-law) tails, rather than Tracy–Widom (Auffinger et al., 2009).

4.4 The √2 Factor: Interpretation 

The √2 improvement from shuffle-once over IID/FlipFlop has an intuitive explanation. Consider what happens during one epoch: • IID sampling: Some examples may be sampled multiple times, while others are missed entirely. On average, a fraction 1/e ≈ 37% of examples are never used in an epoch. • Shuffle-once: Every example is used exactly once per epoch. This ensures more uniform coverage of the data. 16 The shuffle-once method makes better use of the available information by ensuring every example contributes to each epoch. The √2 factor quantifies this advantage precisely. Interestingly, FlipFlop does not share this advantage despite also using every example. The alternating forward-backward pattern creates correlations across epochs that reduce efficiency. In the limit of many epochs, the “flop” (backward) pass provides diminishing additional information.

5 Main Result III: Universal Shuffling Gap Conjecture 

Our analysis thus far reveals a clear separation between two aspects of power-law learning: 1. The Rate Exponent ( β): Determined by the left-edge spectral exponent of the data, as shown by the general theory for IID SGD (Section 3). 2. The Leading Constant: Optimized by the choice of shuffling method, as precisely quantified for isotropic Gaussian data (Section 4). We propose that these two phenomena naturally combine, extending the constant-factor advantage of Single Shuffle to all data distributions exhibiting a power-law spectral edge. 

Conjecture 12 (Universal Shuffling Gap) For any data distribution with a spectral edge 

F (λ) ∼ a λ β (as defined in Theorem 5), the expected test loss for Single Shuffle (SS) and symmetric shuffling schemes like Flip-Flop (FF) and IID sampling are approximated by: Test Loss SS (n) ≈ CSS (β, η ) · n−β , Test Loss F F/IID (n) ≈ CF F/IID (β, η ) · n−β ,

where CSS (β, η ) < C F F/IID (β, η ) for all β > 0 and appropriate step sizes η. Furthermore, the ratio CF F/IID (β, η )/C SS (β, η ) is bounded below by a constant greater than 1, depending only on β.

In essence, we conjecture that while the “spectral source condition” sets the speed limit (the exponent β), the choice of shuffling algorithm consistently optimizes the efficiency (the leading constant) within that limit. A proof of this conjecture would offer a more complete and unified theoretical model for understanding the role of data geometry and algorithmic choices in the efficiency of large-scale optimization.

7 Discussion 

We have extended the spectral theory of power-law convergence from full gradient descent to stochastic gradient descent, establishing that the spectral structure of the data—not the optimization algorithm—determines the convergence rate. 21 7.1 Summary of Contributions General IID SGD bounds. Under the power-law spectral condition ρ((0 , λ ]) ≤ Qλ β , we proved that SGD with IID sampling achieves test loss O(n−β ) and training loss O(n−(β+1) ).This extends the framework of Velikanov and Yarotsky (2024) from full GD to stochastic methods, showing that the rate is determined by spectral density, not by the determinism of gradient computation. 

Exact shuffling constants. For isotropic Gaussian data ( β = 1 /2), we derived exact leading constants for three sampling schemes using free probability theory. The key finding is that shuffle-once achieves a factor of √2 improvement in test loss over both IID sampling and FlipFlop. This is the first tight analysis of shuffling benefits. 

Universality conjecture. We conjectured that the √2 shuffling advantage holds for all spectral exponents β, not just Gaussians, and provided extensive empirical support. 

7.2 Practical Recommendations 

Our results have immediate practical implications: 1. Use shuffle-once. For any SGD application, shuffling the data once at the start and reusing the same order each epoch provides a guaranteed √2 improvement in test loss over IID sampling. This costs nothing computationally. 2. Avoid FlipFlop as a drop-in “shuffling improvement” in the interpolating regime. In the homogeneous/interpolating least-squares setting analyzed here, FlipFlop performs no better than IID sampling in our asymptotics, despite its theoretical appeal (reversing makes the two-epoch map symmetric). The correlations introduced by the backward pass negate the variance reduction from exact coverage. Regime caveat: for inconsistent systems (no exact solution) or for objectives beyond least squares, stabil-ity/robustness considerations can change which schedule is preferable; in those settings one typically uses algorithms designed for inconsistency (e.g. extended/randomized Kaczmarz variants) rather than FlipFlop per se (Strohmer and Vershynin, 2009; Needell et al., 2014; Zouzias and Freris, 2013). 3. Measure the spectrum. The convergence rate depends on the spectral exponent 

β. For a new dataset, computing the eigenvalue CDF of the Gram matrix reveals the expected power-law behavior.

Why observed slopes can be much smaller than 1/2. Our theory predicts that the intermediate power-law slope is set by the left-edge spectral exponent β (test loss ∼ n−β ,training loss ∼ n−(β+1) ). In settings where practitioners report much smaller slopes (e.g. 

0.07 –0.15 ), the framework suggests that the relevant Gram/NTK spectrum has a much heavier left edge (smaller β), for example β = 1 /(d + 1) in the NTK model (yielding β ≈ 0.14 

for d = 6 and β ≈ 0.07 for d = 13 ), or more generally due to strong anisotropy/heavy tails on the spectral edge. 

Representation learning. Modern practice often involves linear probing on pretrained embeddings (e.g., CLIP, ResNet features). These embeddings typically have near-isotropic structure ( β ≈ 1/2), putting them squarely in our framework. 

Frozen-feature Transformers (head-only training). To connect more directly to neural network training, we also study a tiny Transformer language model in a linearized regime where the network body is frozen and only the final linear head is trained (head-only MSE on next-token prediction). In this setting, the loss is quadratic in the trained parameters and the relevant curvature is the feature covariance Cov train . Figure 6 shows that the theory-aligned parameter error and residual are well predicted by the shifted-edge asymptotic once the Laplace window enters the fitted edge regime (here ≳ 10 3 steps). 

Implicit regularization. The power-law convergence we analyze may connect to the implicit regularization properties of SGD. The algorithm’s slow convergence on low-eigenvalue directions acts as a form of spectral regularization, potentially explaining generalization in overparameterized settings. 23 7.4 Limitations 

Our analysis has several limitations: 1. Linear models. We analyze quadratic loss on linear models. Extension to non-convex losses (neural networks) requires different techniques. 2. Constant step size. We assume η is fixed. Adaptive methods (Adam, learning rate schedules) may have different dynamics. 3. No mini-batches. We analyze single-example updates. Mini-batch SGD interpolates between our analysis and full GD. 4. Finite-d constants at shifted edges. Our main theorems are asymptotic in (d, n ).When the edge is shifted ( λ0 > 0) and β ≳ 1, the finite-d gap between λ0 and the smallest observed eigenvalue can materially affect the apparent constants. Appendix A, Proposition 15 gives a simple finite-d correction that improves accuracy in this regime. 5. Universality unproven. Conjecture 12 lacks a complete proof. Extending free probability techniques beyond the Gaussian case is an open challenge. 

7.5 Open Problems 

Several directions merit further investigation: 1. Prove the universality conjecture. Develop techniques to compute shuffling constants for general power-law spectra. 2. Analyze mini-batch SGD. How does batch size affect the power-law exponent and shuffling advantage? Preliminary experiments suggest the exponent is preserved but constants change. 3. Connect to neural network training. Can power-law spectral conditions explain observed convergence rates in deep learning? What determines β for neural network loss landscapes? 4. Optimal shuffling. Is shuffle-once optimal among all data orderings, or could adaptive orderings (curriculum learning) do better? 5. Non-stationary spectra. In deep learning, the effective Gram matrix changes during training. How do time-varying spectra affect convergence? 

7.6 Conclusion 

Power-law convergence is not an artifact of full gradient descent—it is a fundamental property of optimization under power-law spectral conditions. The spectral structure of the data, captured by the exponent β, determines the rate regardless of whether we use deterministic or stochastic updates. The practical message is clear: shuffle-once is the optimal default for SGD. It provides a √2 improvement over IID sampling with no computational cost. This simple recommendation, 24 backed by our theoretical analysis and extensive experiments, offers immediate value for practitioners.
